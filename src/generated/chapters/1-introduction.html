1 Introduction#

Neural networks could be the most consequential artefacts humanity has built, yet we do not understand how they work or reliably control what they do. This is true despite humans designing the training procedure: we specify how models learn, but not what they learn. Interpretability is the effort to understand what they learn and why they behave as they do, rather than only observing what they output. Mechanistic interpretability (MI) is one approach within it. Rather than explaining behaviour from the outside, it reverse-engineers a network into the internal mechanisms that produce that behaviour, which can yield both a richer understanding of the model and more reliable control over it.

Recent decades have seen exponential improvement in AI capabilities ((Touvron et al. 2023); (OpenAI et al. 2024); (Team et al. 2024); (QwenAI 2025); (DeepSeek-AI 2025)) but methods for understanding and explaining internal mechanisms have lagged behind. This has created an interpretability gap between what models can do and what we know about how they do it. The gap is highly relevant to safety. Purely behavioural safety measures such as RLHF (Christiano et al. 2017; Ouyang et al. 2022) and adversarial testing are not robust against sophisticated failure modes such as deceptive alignment (Hubinger et al. 2024).

The interpretability gap is the disparity between what models do (their capabilities and behaviors) and what we understand about how they do it (their internal mechanisms). As models scale, this gap risks widening: reasoning emerges that we cannot yet trace, deceptive behaviors may remain undetected, and safety guarantees built on incomplete understanding may fail unexpectedly.

1.1 Brief History of Interpretability #

Efforts to understand neural network internals began almost as soon as deep learning achieved practical success. Zeiler and Fergus (2014) developed visualization techniques to inspect the convolutional filters that AlexNet (Krizhevsky et al. 2012) had learned. Their study revealed that early layers detected edges while deeper layers responded to increasingly complex patterns. Karpathy et al. (2015) and Li et al. (2016) extended this approach to recurrent networks. By manually inspecting individual neurons they discovered ones tracking various linguistic features such as quotes, line lengths, brackets, negation and syntactic structure. This showed empirically that interpretable structure could emerge without explicit supervision.

This early work was largely manual: researchers examined individual neurons and reported qualitative findings. The field soon developed more systematic approaches.

Probing classifiers, introduced by Alain and Bengio (2017)

and popularized through work on linguistic structure (Shi et al. 2016; Adi et al. 2017; Belinkov et al. 2017; Conneau et al. 2018), offered a way to test whether specific information was encoded in learned representations. Probing asked whether syntactic category, semantic role, or sentence length could be extracted from a layer’s activations. Dalvi et al. (2019) extended it towards automatically identifying individual neurons learning a concept by exploiting the weights of the probing classifier.

This line of work flourished between 2016 and 2019, producing a rich catalog of what information was present in activation patterns. It remains important for interpretability research to consider carefully what conclusions can legitimately be drawn from the presence of information in a network.

The introduction of transformers (Vaswani et al. 2017a) and BERT (Devlin et al. 2019) intensified both the need and the opportunity for interpretability research. Attention weights seemed to offer a direct window into model internals, spawning work on attention visualization (Vig 2019; Clark et al. 2019) and debates over whether attention provides sufficient explanation (Jain and Wallace 2019; Wiegreffe and Pinter 2019). The resulting “BERTology” literature (Rogers et al. 2020) systematically probed transformer internal states, while behavioral evaluations tested linguistic competence with curated datasets.

Feature visualization research by Chris Olah and collaborators at Google Brain, published through the Distill journal (Distill 2017), argued that neural networks contain meaningful, human-interpretable features. OpenAI subsequently developed these ideas into the “Circuits” research program (Olah et al. 2020), which framed neural networks as compositions of interpretable features connected by understandable algorithms. This approach is the foundation of modern mechanistic interpretability.

This framework later generalized to language models, with work on induction heads (Olsson et al. 2022), indirect object identification circuits (Wang et al. 2023), and modular arithmetic in toy models (Nanda et al. 2023; Quirke and Barez 2024). By the end of 2023, sparse autoencoders enabled feature extraction at billion-parameter scale (Bricken et al. 2023; Templeton et al. 2024)—we will discuss whether these methods provide complete mechanistic understanding and contrast them with simpler methods in section 15.

Situating the field.

Work in this area goes by several names. It has been called mechanistic interpretability, network analysis, representation engineering, or simply interpretability, and these terms are often used interchangeably rather than marking sharp distinctions. The scope of the field is still settling, and there is active discussion about what the word “mechanistic” should ask of an explanation (Saphra and Wiegreffe 2024). A growing line of work holds that interpretability should be held to scientific standards, producing claims that are falsifiable, reproducible, and predictive, so that it can act as a form of model evaluation rather than only a diagnostic (Lee et al. 2026; Orgad et al. 2026) Mechanistic work also sits within a longer tradition of interpretability research and connects to several adjacent areas, and recent surveys place it in that broader context (Raukur et al. 2022).

Sparse autoencoders, for example, build directly on decades of work in sparse coding ((Olshausen and Field 1996); (Lewicki and Sejnowski 2000); (Lee et al. 2006); (Ng 2011)), dictionary learning, and signal processing. Similarly, attention-based explanations recapitulate debates from the saliency and feature attribution literature ((Adebayo et al. 2018a); (Seo et al. 2018)).

This handbook therefore aims to situate contemporary mechanistic methods within a longer intellectual landscape that predates work that become popular post 2020.

Curiosity and discovery.

Mechanistic interpretability is worth pursuing for reasons beyond safety. Part of the appeal is ordinary scientific curiosity. We have built systems that solve problems we cannot fully specify, and wanting to know how such a system works is among the oldest motivations in science, whether the object is a cell, a brain, or a trained neural network. Neural networks are an unusual case, because unlike a biological brain their entire internal state is available to read and to change. That access makes otherwise hard questions approachable, such as what a model represents internally or whether a particular concept is encoded in it at all. There is also the prospect of genuine discovery. A model trained on a difficult task may settle on solutions its designers did not anticipate, and recovering those internal structures can surface knowledge the model has in effect found on its own, from compact algorithms to representations of the world implicit in its predictions. Understood this way, interpretability is a way of learning from a model, not just auditing it.

Safety.

Many studies in mechanistic interpretability are motivated by AI safety ((Burns et al. 2023; Garcı́a-Carrasco et al. 2024); (Templeton et al. 2024)).

Researchers hope that understanding model internals will enable detection of deception, identification of dangerous capabilities, or reliable control of model behavior. However, it is unclear whether current mechanistic methods can address the failure modes that safety researchers care about most or enable interventions that would be impossible using purely observational or correlation-based

approaches. This concern motivates us to assess methods, not only by the understanding they provide, but also by the actions they enable.

Building on this history, recent interpretability research reflects two significant methodological shifts.

First, from manual to assisted analysis. At current model sizes, manual inspection of features and circuits is impractical. Consequently, researchers have developed methods that automate parts of mechanistic interpretability research. In particular, sparse autoencoders are a key automation mechanism: without supervision they extract millions of independent features from model activations (Templeton et al. 2024; Bricken et al. 2023). Other work has proposed pipelines to automatically identify candidate circuits (Conmy et al. 2023), use language models to generate natural-language feature explanations ((Paulo et al. 2025); (Bills et al. 2023; Foote et al. 2023)), and provide initial validation of mechanistic hypotheses. While human judgment remains essential, some automation has become a necessary.

Second, divergence in research priorities. Even within safety driven mechanistic interpretability research, there are distinct approaches. Some prioritize immediate safety interventions such as steering, monitoring, and concept erasure, without requiring complete mechanistic understanding (Nanda et al. 2025). While others prioritize complete causal understanding of model computation, even where this requires substantial resources (Gao 2025). Separate threads emphasize automating interpretability workflows to enable domain experts to query and correct model behavior (Barez 2026), studying learning dynamics to anticipate emergent capabilities before deployment (Hoogland et al. 2023), or exploring whether models can serve as introspective agents that explain their own internals (Kim et al. 2025).

These divergent research directions also reflect complexity of the problem and how many unknowns still exist in understanding the inner working of these models. Bascially, there are two many un-answered questions from diverse research orientations. These divergent research directions reflect genuine uncertainty about what interpretability should deliver and what counts as success. Rather than advocating for any single approach, this handbook organises recent progress around shared technical foundations (Section 5 onwards) and makes explicit the trade-offs between different goals (Section 13). We aim to clarify how different approaches differ in their assumptions, capabilities, and failure modes. This provides a principled basis for selecting and combining methods according to one’s scientific and practical objectives.

Useful Surveys that we bild on.

Several surveys cover overlapping territory. Bereska and Gavves (2024) and Zhang et al. (2026) survey mechanistic interpretability methods with a focus on safety applications. Belinkov and Glass (2019) provide a broad primer on neural language model interpretability. H. Zhao et al. (2024) survey explainability and interpretability for large language models. Rai et al. (2024) provide a task-centric, beginner-friendly taxonomy and review of mechanistic interpretability for Transformer-based language models, while Ferrando et al. (2024) offer a technical primer on interpreting the inner workings and known mechanisms of Transformer-based language models. More specifically, Sajjad et al. (2022) survey neuron-level interpretation, covering methods to discover, interpret, and control individual neurons. At a similar granularity, Shu et al. (2025) survey Sparse Autoencoders (SAE), covering different SAE architectures, feature explanation methods, evaluation, and applications.

Our survey differs from previous surveys in framing, scope and emphasis.

First, we take a critical stance on the relationship between interpretability and safety. Much recent work assumes that mechanistic understanding will translate to safety benefits, but this connection remains underspecified. We evaluate methods not only by the understanding they provide, but by whether they enable safety-relevant actions—detecting deception, identifying dangerous capabilities, correcting failures—that simpler approaches cannot.

Second, we update technical coverage through early 2025, including recent work on attribution graphs, crosscoders, and large-scale feature analysis, while organising this material around methodological principles rather than individual papers.

Third, we situate contemporary “mechanistic” methods within a longer intellectual lineage, connecting sparse autoencoders to sparse coding (Olshausen and Field 1996), activation patching to causal mediation analysis (Vig et al. 2020), and circuit analysis to earlier work on single-node analysis (Clark et al. 2019). This framing clarifies what is actually novel and what recapitulates prior debates.

Fourth, we address terminological confusion directly. The term “mechanistic interpretability” is used inconsistently across the literature (Saphra and Wiegreffe 2024). The deeper source of the confusion is that it is attached to claims of very different strength, ranging from showing that an intervention changes a behaviour to showing that a computation has been fully reverse-engineered. We treat this as a question about claims rather than about the word. For each method we ask what causal claim its evidence can actually support, place that claim on an explicit scale of strength, and state the assumptions each level requires. A reader can then judge what is being asserted regardless of which label an author uses. This removes most of the apparent disagreement, because the disagreement is usually about the claim and not the term.

Fifth, we provide systematic coverage of evaluation practices. Interpretability methods are often validated through intuitive plausibility rather than rigorous testing. We survey emerging evaluation frameworks, identify gaps, and argue that evaluation methodology and bechmarks related to safety to be among the field’s most pressing open problems.

1.2 Hnadbook Scope#

We survey AI mechanistic interpretability, which is a way of understanding the internal computations of neural networks—how models transform inputs into outputs through their intermediate representations and components. In particular, mechanistic analysis involves direct study or manipulation of model internals to establish causal claims about computation. This involves:

  1. Engagement with model internals: Analysis targets intermediate representations, individual components (neurons, attention heads, layers), or learned parameters—not just input-output relationships.

  2. Causal claims: Methods support interventions (ablation, patching, steering) that establish cause-effect relationships, not merely correlations.

This distinguishes it from other approaches to understanding neural networks, such as behavioral evaluation (which outputs models produce) and input attribution (which inputs matter). However, interpretability methods that are not themselves mechanistic can facilitate mechanistic analysis. Probing classifiers identify what information is encoded in representations; this can become mechanistic when the identified directions are used for steering or ablation. Attribution methods locate salient components; intervening on those components tests causal hypotheses. Sparse autoencoders decompose complex representations into interpretable features that enable targeted intervention. The key distinction is between methods that describe (correlational) and methods that intervene (causal)—but descriptive methods often provide the targets for intervention. Therefore, we cover in depth methods that analyse internal computation and that we can treat within a shared causal vocabulary; we treat more briefly methods that are either behavioural or that, while mechanistic, need a separate theoretical apparatus.

We cover the following in depth:

  • Theoretical foundations: Core hypotheses underlying mechanistic analysis—linear representations, superposition, privileged access (Section 5)

  • Representation analysis: Probing classifiers, sparse autoencoders, dictionary learning, representation similarity (Sections 6, 8, 12)

  • Causal methods: Activation patching, causal tracing, circuit analysis, steering and ablation (Sections 7)

  • Training dynamics: Developmental interpretability, phase transitions, learning dynamics (Section 10)

  • Training data attribution: Influence functions, data provenance (Section 9)

  • Evaluation: Assessing validity of interpretability claims (Section 7.6)

  • Safety applications: From understanding to intervention (Section 14)

We discuss but do not cover in depth:

  • Behavioral evaluation: Red-teaming, benchmarks, capability evaluations ((Ganguli et al. 2022); (Perez, Huang, F. Song, et al. 2022)). These studies assess what models do without explaining how. Section 3 discusses when behavioral methods may or may not suffice.

  • Input attribution: SHAP (Lundberg and Lee 2017), LIME (Ribeiro et al. 2016), integrated gradients (Sundararajan et al. 2017), saliency maps (Simonyan et al. 2014). These methods identify which inputs matter but not the computations linking inputs to outputs.

  • Concept-based models:

    This class of models,especially Concept Bottleneck Models (CBMs) (Koh et al. 2020), constrain models to route computation through human-specified concepts. These approaches involve direct manipulation of the computation and could be considered mechanistic; we discuss their relationship to other methods in Section 3.2.

  • Causal abstraction: Work on aligning model computations to high-level causal graphs (Geiger et al. 2021). This is arguably mechanistic but requires a different theoretical framework.
