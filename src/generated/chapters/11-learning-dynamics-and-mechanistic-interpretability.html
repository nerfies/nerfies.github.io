11 Learning Dynamics and Mechanistic Interpretability#

Different modules and parameters converge at different rates (Raghu et al. 2017), so mechanisms may be understood temporally through their development—Saphra (2023) even posited that, like biological species, they can be understood only through their development. Interpretability research in empirical training dynamics therefore expresses two interconnected objectives: First, research aims at scientifically understanding the developmental history of internal mechanisms or concepts. Second, researchers leverage this history as a tool for identifying or understanding those mechanisms. While we focus on the latter use of training as a method for identifying and understanding mechanisms, this understanding crucially comes from the former research into how such mechanisms form.

11.1 Mechanism Discovery from Training Dynamics#

Despite our limited understanding of circuit formation, developmental evidence is increasingly used to confirm or analyze the role of specific features and circuits. However, we can only point to a few recent works that use temporal analysis as a tool for circuit discovery.

Ge et al. (2026) used cross-coders between checkpoints to identify which linear features are learned over a particular period. Kangaslahti et al. (2026), meanwhile, clustered datapoints’ decomposed loss curves to recover concepts that are learned suddenly along particular linear directions.

11.2 Mechanism Evolution During Training#

Training can be studied through pure observations or through causal interventions. Ideally, to support a claim that a capability depends on some internal mechanism, we employ both. Olsson et al. (2022), for example, found that the formation of induction heads coincided with a breakthrough in in-context learning—and that enforcing the induction head structure accelerated this same breakthrough. Similarly, A. Chen et al. (2024) found that internal syntactic structures in specific Transformer heads (Voita et al. 2019; Clark et al. 2019) directly preceded a breakthrough in modeling complex grammatical phenomena—and that suppressing the internal syntactic structures also delayed the consecutive capabilities breakthrough.

This subsection examines how specific internal mechanisms—circuits, features, attention patternsform, change, and sometimes disappear over the course of training, treating the trajectory of model internals as a first-class object of study.

11.2.3 Phase changes#

Induction heads and syntactic attention structure share a common property: both involve Transformer heads specializing in specific operations. Both structures therefore emerge abruptly in a symmetry-breaking phase transition, during which the model selects parameters to store each mechanism. In fact, both structures appear during the same symmetry-breaking phase transition, after which language modeling loss decreases rapidly but language model perplexity begins to diverge from implicit human judgments recovered by cognitive scientists (Aoyama and Wilcox 2025).

Because phase transitions signal the formation of internal modular structures, they are a phenomenon of great interest to many mechanistic interpretability researchers. They are one of three indicators of modular mechanistic structure and resulting compositional generalization: training phase transitions, emergent scaling trends, and heterogeneous clustering across random seeds (Rosie Zhao et al. 2024).

Because of the relationship between phase transitions and emergence, they hold particular interest to safety researchers in general because they make model capabilities unpredictable through scaling laws (Hestness et al. 2017; Rosenfeld et al. 2019; Kaplan et al. 2020). If we cannot predict the capabilities of a model before fully training it, we might not predict the sudden appearance of reward hacking or other misaligned behaviors. This connection makes phase transitions particularly salient to safety, compared to other interpretable signatures.

11.3 Phase Changes and Grokking#

Some of the most striking phenomena in learning dynamics involve qualitative shifts in model behavior or internal structure that occur abruptly relative to the overall training process; we review theoretical and empirical work on these phase changes and their implications for safety

11.3.1 Grokking#

Grokking—the phenomenon where models suddenly generalize long after memorizing a training set—has become a key case study for mechanistic accounts of phase transitions, with work showing that generalizing circuits form gradually alongside memorization circuits before abruptly overtaking them.

11.3.2 Singular Learning Theory#

Singular learning theory provides a mathematical framework rooted in algebraic geometry for understanding phase transitions in learning, offering potential theoretical foundations for predicting when and why qualitative shifts in model internals occur, though its empirical payoff for mech interp specifically remains nascent.

11.3.3 Modeling Phase Changes#

We discuss work on building models of phase changes to advance our understanding of how they happen. “Hidden Breakthroughs in Language Model Training” amd “ Latent state models of training dynamics” are two examples.

11.5 Intervening on Models During Training#

Typically we are most invested in the specific behaviors of models at the end of training since that’s what is actually deployed. Viewing models as time-evolving objects enables us to talk about how we can monitor and intervene to suppress undesirable behaviors before training finishes.

11.5.1 Train-Time Interventions#

The basic concept here is that you have some behavior or property that you’re monitoring and when you decide it goes outside the acceptable range of whatever you do something to the training process to steer it back in. This can be done in pretraining, midtraining, or posttraining, but the typical posttraining set-up is “here’s a model behavior we don’t like, lets finetune to fix it” which is not in scope.
