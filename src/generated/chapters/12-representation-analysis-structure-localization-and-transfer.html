12 Representation Analysis: Structure, Localization, and Transfer#

12.1 Model Diffing: Tracking Representational Changes Across Models#

Many concerning behaviors, such as reward hacking or sycophancy, arise during finetuning ((Sharma et al. 2024); (MacDiarmid et al. 2025)). This makes it important to monitor features and circuits that have been introduced or amplified during this process to prevent unsafe models from being deployed. Reliable model diffing may help to surface some such features.

For two models, model diffing seeks to identify differences in features or circuits between them. These differences may consist of features or circuits that are exclusive to one model, or of differences in how shared features or circuits are used.

Representation alignment and convergent learning. An earlier line of work compared internal representations across models by measuring statistical similarity between neurons or subspaces, using correlation or mutual information (Li et al. 2015), canonical correlation–based methods such as SVCCA and PWCCA (Raghu et al. 2017; Morcos et al. 2018b), or kernel-based alignment metrics like CKA (Kornblith et al. 2019). Related approaches study whether subsets of neurons in one model can be expressed as linear combinations of neurons in another (Wang et al. 2018). This literature primarily aims to characterize convergent learning: whether independently trained models (often only differing by weight initialisation) learn similar representations up to permutation, rotation, or linear reparameterization. In contrast to model diffing as defined here, these methods typically focus on representational similarity rather than on identifying features or circuits that are introduced, amplified, or repurposed by a specific training intervention.
Applications. Model diffing has been applied to assess the effect of finetuning ((Minder et al. 2025); (Lindsey et al. 2024; M. Wang et al. 2025; Aranguri and McGrath 2025)) and distillation (Baek and Tegmark 2025), to track differences across model architectures (Thasarathan et al. 2025; Jiralerspong and Bricken 2025), and to study the evolution of concepts during pretraining ((Bayazit et al. 2025; Ge et al. 2025)).
Dictionary learning methods. Dictionary learning is a natural approach to model diffing in a hypothesis-free way. This may highlight newly introduced features of concern that are unexpected. When diffing models of the same architecture, e.g. a base and chat-finetuned model, dictionary learning methods, like SAEs or transcoders, may be trained on the difference in token-activations, so-called Diff-SAEs  (Dumas et al. 2025). This difference should capture at least some of the features that finetuning introduced. Alternatively, one can train an SAE on the base model alone and use it as a fixed feature basis for comparison. Differences in latent activations between the base and finetuned models are then measured on a shared evaluation prompt set (M. Wang et al. 2025). By causally intervening with latents that activate more strongly in the finetuned model, it is possible to identify directions responsible for misaligned behavior (M. Wang et al. 2025).
One challenge for any model-diffing approach is to attribute features in the representations of a finetuned model to the finetuning itself. To solve this problem, an SAE trained on a base model can be finetuned stage-wise  (Bricken et al. 2024): first on the base model with finetuning data, then on the finetuned model with base data and finally on the finetuned model with finetuning data. Only features exclusive to the last setting are likely to have been introduced via finetuning.
SAEs can be applied to model-diffing when inputs from different models have the same dimension. When diffing two models of a different architecture, e.g. to track dispositions across model generations or across sizes, this might not be the case; in that scenario, crosscoders offer an alternative (Figure 14). Crosscoders can define model-specific encoders, with arbitrarily different input dimensions, which then project into one or more shared representational spaces. Note that even though the main advantage of crosscoders over SAEs is cross-architecture comparisons, most prior work has applied them to base and finetuned models ((Lindsey et al. 2024); (Bricken et al. 2024); (Minder et al. 2025)), except for Thasarathan et al. (2025) and Jiralerspong and Bricken (2025).
A related approach is to model differences in how representations are transformed, rather than comparing the representations themselves. (Hu et al. 2026) use transcoders to learn a function that approximates the difference in MLP computation between a base model and its finetuned variant. Concretely, adapters are trained such that a base MLP augmented with a learned correction term reproduces the behavior of the finetuned MLP within the full model. Unlike Diff-SAEs, which operate on activation differences, this method learns a transformation that approximates how finetuning modifies the model’s MLP computation.

Dictionary learning methods for model-diffing risk falsely identifying model-exclusive features (Minder et al. 2025; Jiralerspong and Bricken 2025). This can result from the loss function: for instance, Minder et al. (2025) showed that crosscoders trained with an L1 loss on hidden states can falsely identify novel features, which they try to mitigate with the BatchTopK activation function. However, even with improved architecture, a risk of false positives may persist. During finetuning, the direction representing some feature can rotate, and dictionary learning methods currently cannot distinguish between features that are introduced via finetuning as opposed to merely represented differently. Thus, supposedly model-exclusive features should be regarded as hypotheses that need further validation.

LLM-based approaches. To diff closed-weight models, it is desirable to develop model diffing tools that just rely on API access. To that end, Kempf et al. (2026) show that an LLM can generate natural-language hypotheses about behavioural differences between two models by extracting, clustering, and summarising differences across many response pairs from the two models on a shared prompt set. Benchmarked against an SAE-based baseline, the LLM-based method matches it on many dimensions while surfacing more abstract, behaviour-level differences.

12.2 Knowledge Localization: Where Is Information Stored?#

Another fundamental question in mechanistic interpretability is where specific knowledge resides within a model. Causal tracing identifies which attention heads or MLP directions are causally responsible for particular facts or reasoning tasks by measuring how intervening on those components affects output behavior (Meng et al. 2022). Complementary approaches use rank analysis to quantify information flow through specific components via singular value decomposition of activation matrices, revealing that some facts and reasoning patterns concentrate in low-rank subspaces of feed-forward layers.

However, this localization perspective has fundamental limitations. Knowledge is often distributed across many parameters rather than concentrated in isolated components, particularly in large overparameterized models where redundancy is common. This distributed structure makes precise localization difficult and suggests that fully understanding where knowledge lives may require thinking in terms of distributed representations and circuits rather than isolated anatomical locations. Consequently, knowledge localization is most useful when combined with mechanistic analysis of how multiple components interact to implement computation.

To illustrate knowledge localization in practice, one can examine how behaviorally relevant directions manifest across layers and components. For example:

  • Refusal vectors \mathbf{r}^\ell: projecting residual activations h_t^\ell onto \mathbf{r}^\ell, s_t^\ell = \langle h_t^\ell, \mathbf{r}^\ell \rangle, quantifies which layers or MLP subspaces encode the model’s tendency to decline harmful prompts. Peaks in s_t^\ell indicate components that strongly contribute to refusal, while smaller distributed values reveal how the signal spreads across the network.

  • Sleeper agent probe directions \mathbf{d}^\ell: similar projections reveal which layers or components carry latent backdoor objectives. Comparing s_t^\ell across layers or heads highlights concentrated versus distributed storage of these conditional behaviors.

These analyses demonstrate that knowledge or behavior can be partially localized, but often resides in a combination of concentrated subspaces and distributed representations, reinforcing the need to complement localization with mechanistic circuit analysis.

12.3 Representation Engineering and Steering#

Overview.

Representation engineering aims to control model behavior by intervening directly in activation space rather than modifying weights. A prominent class of methods, generally defined as activation steering, biases model outputs by adding carefully chosen directions to intermediate activations—most commonly the residual stream—during inference. These interventions are lightweight, require no retraining, and are therefore attractive for rapid behavioral control in deployed systems.

A steering vector is a direction v \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{hidden}}} in activation space such that adding \alpha v to a layer activation biases the model toward a target behavior. In practice, v is estimated by contrasting activations associated with different behaviors, e.g., taking differences of class-conditional means: v = \mathbb{E}[h | \text{preferred}] - \mathbb{E}[h | \text{unpreferred}] or by extracting discriminative directions using linear methods such as CCA (Rimsky et al. 2024). Application is simple: at inference, replace h_\ell with h_\ell + \alpha v to shift behavior without retraining.

Despite their simplicity, steering vectors reliably induce behaviors like improved helpfulness, refusal suppression, or stylistic changes (Rimsky et al. 2024; D. Tan et al. 2024; Zhao et al. 2025).

Optimization-Based Steering.

Beyond closed-form constructions, steering directions can be obtained by directly optimizing objectives over activations—maximizing task performance or suppressing undesired outputs under intervention. These optimization-based approaches allow greater flexibility and adapt to complex, non-linear objectives, but incur higher computational cost (Dunefsky and Cohan 2025; Oozeer, Marks, et al. 2025).

Relation to Causal Evaluation of Feature Extraction Methods.

Representation engineering is closely related to causal analysis for evaluating feature extraction methods such as SAEs. In particular, to assess whether features claimed to encode specific semantic meanings genuinely influence model behavior in those ways, one can intervene on the model’s internal representations by steering along these features and observing the resulting changes in output text. Such intervention-based evaluations directly measure the causal effects of extracted features on model outputs.

This paradigm enables quantitative comparisons of feature quality across different extraction methods, such as SAEs, difference-in-mean, and DAS. Benchmarks designed for this purpose include, but are not limited to, RAVEL (Huang et al. 2024), MIB (Mueller, Geiger, et al. 2025), and AxBench (Wu et al. 2025).

Despite effectiveness, steering vectors have critical limitations. First, they often fail to generalize across distribution shifts: a vector derived for one task may degrade in another. Second, behavioral success does not guarantee mechanistic transparency. A steering intervention may activate multiple features, interact nonlinearly with existing computation, or exploit spurious pathways that yield desired outputs without corresponding to clean circuit-level modifications. As a result, steering remains primarily a behavioral tool rather than a mechanistic one.

Two concrete examples of steering vectors appear in earlier sections:

  • Refusal vectors \mathbf{r}^\ell (Definition 8.3): manipulating this direction modulates the model’s tendency to decline harmful prompts.

  • Sleeper agent probe directions \mathbf{d}^\ell (Example 8.6.9): projections onto this direction reveal latent backdoor behavior and can in principle steer activations toward or away from the hidden objective.

These examples illustrate how steering vectors correspond to interpretable directions in residual space along which the model’s behavior can be predictably influenced.
