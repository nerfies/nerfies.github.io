15 Open Problems#

This section identifies fundamental open questions that mechanistic interpretability (MI) has raised but not perfectly answered or solved. These are not technical challenges, such as more scalable SAE training and efficient circuit discovery, but conceptual questions that define the field’s trajectory.

15.1 When Is Mechanistic Understanding Necessary?#

A foundational question remains unresolved: for which problems and scenarios is mechanistic understanding required versus when behavioral methods suffice? Evidence cuts both ways. RLHF and adversarial training have substantially improved deployed model safety, yet the sleeper agents experiment (Hubinger et al. 2024) revealed deceptive behaviors hidden from behavioral evaluation while remaining mechanistically detectable (Hubinger et al. 2024; Ouyang et al. 2022). Behavioral robustness degrades under distribution shift and worsens as models scale, suggesting that mechanistic understanding becomes necessary for safety at the frontier scale (Hendrycks et al. 2021).

The most likely answer is that both are necessary: behavioral methods handle broad coverage, mechanistic analysis targets hidden misalignment that behavioral testing cannot reliably detect from the surface (Nanda et al. 2025). Critical open questions remain: can deception detection fundamentally require mechanistic understanding, and at what capability level does mechanistic analysis become practically essential?

15.2 How to Evaluate Mechanistic Interpretability Methods#

Standardized evaluation for MI techniques and findings are usually hard. SAE variants are compared on different metrics; circuit discovery methods optimize for different objectives (size, coverage, interpretability); feature explanations are often evaluated on plausibility but not ground truth. Without standardized metrics, it is unclear whether improvements are real or artifacts of metric choice.

15.2.1 Proposed Evaluation Framework#

We propose that MI methods and findings should be evaluated across four dimensions: Interpretability—are mechanisms understandable to humans? Completeness—what fraction of behavior is explained? Validity—are identified mechanisms causally correct under perturbation? Efficiency—what is the computational cost? Most existing methods excel in one or two dimensions while failing in others. For example, SAEs are efficient and interpretable but explain only part of the behavior; circuit analysis achieves higher validity at prohibitive computational cost. A mature framework requires methods that either succeed in all dimensions or provide clear trade-off characterization.

15.2.2 Standardized Benchmarks#

Progress in MI is partially hindered by the lack of standardized evaluation. A shared benchmark suite would enable direct method comparison and accelerate research. Ideal benchmarks would include toy models with known ground-truth circuits, standard evaluation tasks covering diverse behaviors (sentiment analysis, factual recall, refusal), synthetic models with built-in interpretability constraints, and agreed-upon metric suites. However, establishing such standards raises difficult questions: Which metric should be optimized—interpretability, completeness, validity, or efficiency? Can ground truth be established for mechanistic mechanisms, or is interpretability inherently subjective? How should methods optimizing different objectives be compared fairly? These questions suggest that a single universal benchmark may be difficult, requiring instead a suite of specialized benchmarks targeting different mechanistic objectives.

15.3 Scalability and Adversarial Robustness#

A common and critical bottleneck in many existing MI approaches is scalability to model size, model diversity, and input domains. Whether this bottleneck can be overcome through algorithmic improvements or represents a fundamental limitation remains unclear. Second, adversarial obfuscation exists: if an MI tool is logged and introduced online, deployed frontier models, which consistently learn new information from the internet, may optimize to encode information in ways that resist interpretation by the tool, such as distributing computation across superposition, implementing behaviorally equivalent but mechanistically opaque circuits, or hiding information in entangled features (Gao 2025; Nanda et al. 2025), analogous to the phenomenon of evaluation awareness (Anthropic 2025a). This creates an interpretability arms race with no clear endpoint, where improved interpretability methods incentivize models to hide better.

Underlying both challenges is a deeper question: can mechanistic explanations be verified as genuine mechanisms rather than post-hoc rationalizations? Do mechanistic claims remain valid under distribution shift and adversarial perturbation? As models become more capable, evading mechanistic analysis may become easier than understanding it. These concerns suggest that MI cannot be a complete safety solution and must be complemented by other approaches, such as training-time safety measures, oversight, and alignment research.

15.4 Mechanistic Understanding and Alignment#

MI provides tools for behavioral control. For example, steering vectors can bias outputs in desired directions, knowledge editing can modify factual beliefs, and circuit analysis can support targeted interventions (Nanda et al. 2025). However, these methods do not directly address alignment specification: understanding a circuit that enforces refusals or constraints allows auditing and intervention, but does not indicate what the model should value or whether its behavior genuinely reflects human intent.

Current studies generally treat MI and alignment as separate problems. Mechanistic methods support verification and control, while other approaches, such as constitutional AI (Bai, Kadavath, et al. 2022) and value learning (Bai, Jones, et al. 2022), focus on specifying desired behaviors. Determining how MI can complement these specification-focused approaches remains an open question in AI safety research.

15.5 Interpretation vs. Truth: The Ontology Question#

A foundational question in MI is: what does it mean to mechanistically understand a neural network? This question is not only technical but also conceptual. For example, labeling a feature as a “golden retriever detector” or describing an attention head as “copying the previous token” reflects human choices and abstractions. The same input-output behavior can be realized by multiple mechanistically distinct internal structures, each consistent with the observed causal relationships.

MI research typically assumes that human-interpretable descriptions correspond to some causal structures in the model. However, several challenges arise. Feature naming is subjective: different observers may assign different names to the same learned direction based on their interpretation of the activating examples. The level of abstraction is also a matter of choice: a circuit could be described in terms of copying, attention routing, or low-rank decomposition, depending on human priorities. Representation ambiguity means that multiple mechanisms can produce identical behavior while differing internally. Moreover, intuitive human-language explanations may not always accurately capture the internal computations of the model (Hewitt et al. 2025).

These issues highlight conceptual questions about mechanistic understanding rather than technical limitations. For instance, how should abstraction level be chosen? Can confidence in a mechanistic explanation be established when multiple descriptions are consistent with the same behavior? Should one expect a unique mechanistic description, or are multiple valid interpretations acceptable? Addressing these questions can help clarify the goals and limits of MI research.

15.6 The Proxy Fidelity Problem#

As architectural interventions gain traction (Section 6.9), a new verification challenge emerges: when interpretability is achieved through proxy models, such as sparse networks trained to mimic dense model representations, how can we guarantee that the proxy’s circuits reflect the same mechanisms used by the original model, rather than behaviorally equivalent but mechanistically distinct simulations?

Problem formulation.

Let h^{l, x}_{\text{dense}} be the activation of a dense model f_{\text{dense}} at layer l given input x, and h^{l, x}_{\text{sparse}} be the reconstructed output of a weight-sparse proxy f_{\text{sparse}} with h^{l, x}_{\text{dense}} as input, trained to minimize: \begin{equation*} \mathcal{L}_{\text{proxy}} = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}} \!\left[ \sum_{l} \left\| h^{l, x}_{\text{dense}} - h^{l, x}_{\text{sparse}} \right\|^{2} \right], \tag{5} \end{equation*} where \mathcal{D} is a text corpus. Even if \mathcal{L}_{\text{proxy}} is small and \mathcal{D} is large, the sparse model can achieve the low reconstruction error by a different internal computation. Two models can produce identical outputs (or even identical intermediate activations) while implementing distinct algorithms—a form of mechanistic underdetermination.

Why this matters for safety.

If we identify a “deception circuit” in the sparse proxy and ablate it, we need confidence that (1) the corresponding mechanism exists in the dense model (not just an artifact of the sparse approximation), (2) interventions on the proxy transfer reliably to the dense model, and (3) the proxy does not introduce spurious circuits absent from the original. Although preliminary studies have suggested that interventions on sparse proxies can induce behavioral changes in dense models (Gao, Rajaram, et al. 2025), systematic validation remains limited.

Potential approaches.

Several directions could be pursued to improve proxy fidelity. One approach is causal correspondence testing, which verifies that ablating a circuit in the proxy and the corresponding components in the dense model produces matched behavioral changes across different inputs. The second approach is activation patching transfer, which evaluates whether activations identified as causally important in the proxy retain their causal influence when the same interventions are applied to the dense model. A third direction involves developing bounds of mechanistic divergence: theoretical frameworks that upper-bound how much internal computation can differ between models given constraints like activation reconstruction error.

15.7 Transferability Beyond Transformers#

The methods surveyed in this paper are predominantly developed for and evaluated on transformer architectures. While the foundational “circuits” work (Olah et al. 2020) originated on convolutional networks (InceptionV1 (Szegedy et al. 2015)), demonstrating feature visualization and curve/texture detector circuits, the subsequent MI toolkits, such as SAEs, transcoders, model editing, circuit discovery, and activation patching, have been developed almost exclusively for transformer residual streams and attention mechanisms.

This raises an open question: to what extent do MI methods and findings transfer to other architectures?

  • Convolutional networks: The spatial locality of convolutions may make circuits more naturally modular, but SAE-style decomposition of CNN activations remains underexplored at scale.

  • State space models (e.g., Mamba): These architectures lack attention but implement sequential processing via recurrent dynamics. Whether superposition, linear representation, and circuit-style analysis apply is unknown.

  • Reinforcement learning agents: RL policies involve temporal credit assignment and value estimation, which may require different interpretability primitives than simply next-token prediction. Limited work exists on MuZero and Atari agents, but systematic MI is lacking.

  • Diffusion models: The iterative denoising process introduces temporal structure distinct from autoregressive generation. Emerging work explores attention patterns in diffusion transformers, but feature extraction methods are nascent.

The five research orientations (Pragmatic, Ambitious, Developmental, Agentic, Automated) discussed in Section 13 are framed architecture-agnostically, but their instantiation has been transformer-specific. Validating whether these orientations and the methods they employ generalize to other architectures is necessary before MI can claim broad applicability to AI safety. Until such a validation exists, practitioners should be cautious about extrapolating transformer-derived insights to other neural network architectures.
