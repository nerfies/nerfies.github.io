4 Foundational Concepts for Mechanistic Interpretability#

This section introduces core conceptual primitives used throughout mechanistic interpretability. We deliberately avoid committing to specific hypotheses about how representations are organized or how general these structures are. Instead, we establish a shared vocabulary and computational picture that will be used throughout the survey. In Section 5 we discuss issues with some of the hypotheses in this section.

4.1 Behavioral vs. Mechanistic Understanding#

Behavioral interpretability is often sufficient for evaluating the input-output behavior, but safety-critical applications require mechanistic analysis (as we reviewed in Section 2). The distinction between these methods is the difference between describing a system’s behavior and reverse engineering its internal mechanism.

Behavioral interpretability studies a model f_\theta : \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y} exclusively through input–output pairs (x, f_\theta(x)), treating it as a black box.

Mechanistic interpretability studies a model f_\theta : \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y} by examining its internal structure: the parameters \theta and the intermediate activations \{h_l(x)\}_{l=1}^{L} that together implement a particular behavior keeping the input-output fixed.

4.2 Polysemanticity in Neural Representations#

Consider the activation of a single unit h_l^{(i)}(x) at layer l. Interpreting units would be easier if each one responded to a single understandable concept. But researchers have found that—as by product of how we train models—individual units often activate strongly for multiple, unrelated concepts (Elhage, Hume, Olsson, Nanda, Henighan, Bai, et al. 2022). This phenomena is known as “polysemanticity”.

A unit h_l^{(i)} is polysemantic if any two inputs x, x' \in \mathcal{X}, corresponding to semantically distinct concepts, both produce high absolute activation: |h_l^{(i)}(x)| \gg 0 and |h_l^{(i)}(x')| \gg 0.

Polysemanticity complicates neuron-level explanations and suggests that human-interpretable semantic content may not be aligned with neurons (the standard basis vectors of the representation space). This motivates the search for alternative representational units beyond individual neurons.

4.3 Features as Representational Units#

Since individual units h_l^{(i)} are often polysemantic, mechanistic interpretability seeks alternative units of analysis. The largley agreed upon hypothesis in the community is that models often encode interpretable concepts as directions in activation space, called features.

Features may be distributed across many neurons (Elhage, Hume, Olsson, Nanda, Henighan, Bai, et al. 2022; Bricken et al. 2023; Templeton et al. 2024) and only become apparent when analyzing the representation space as a whole.

A feature is a direction \phi \in \mathbb{R}^d in activation space. The scalar \phi^\top h_l(x) measures the activation (or presence) of that feature for input x. A high value indicates the corresponding concept is represented in the model’s internal.

At this stage, we remain agnostic about the precise mathematical form features take or how they are encoded within the model. Accordingly, we do not assume a specific representational geometry or encoding scheme, and treat existing approaches to identifying or manipulating features as operational approximations shaped by methodological constraints. These representational hypotheses are examined explicitly in Section 5.

Within this operational perspective, features can be recovered or manipulated using different methodological families, including readout-based methods, sparse decomposition techniques, and causal intervention strategies. In practice, most such approaches operationalise features as approximately linear or linearized components of internal representations. Specific examples include probes, dictionary learning methods, and activation-based interventions ((Belinkov 2022; Wang et al. 2023); (Bricken et al. 2023)).

4.4 Transformer Computation#

Transformers (Vaswani et al. 2017b) provide a particularly transparent architecture for mechanistic analysis due to their modular structure and additive information flow.

All transformer submodules read from and write to a shared residual stream, enabling information to persist and be incrementally transformed across layers.

The residual stream is the sequence of activation vectors \{h_l(x)\}_{l=0}^{L} that serve as the shared communication channel between transformer layers. Each sublayer reads from and writes additively to this stream: \begin{align*} h'_l(x) &= h_{l-1}(x) + \text{Attn}_l(h_{l-1}(x)) \\ h_l(x) &= h'_l(x) + \text{MLP}_l(h'_l(x)) \end{align*} This additive structure means information persists by default—sublayers only need to write changes, not copy forward what they wish to preserve which makes targed intervention more reliable.

Let h \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d} denote the residual stream at a given layer. A transformer component c (such as an attention head or an MLP block) is a function c : h \mapsto \Delta h_c(h), whose output is added to the residual stream. The forward computation of a transformer layer can be written as h_\ell = h_{\ell-1} + \sum_{c \in \mathcal C_\ell} \Delta h_c(h_{\ell-1}), where \mathcal C_\ell is the set of components in layer \ell.

This additive decomposition induces a computational structure in which model behavior arises from the combined contributions of individual components acting on shared internal representations.

The attention mechanisms primarily move and combine information across positions, while feedforward (MLP) layers perform nonlinear transformations. This separation of computation enables localized analysis of information routing and transformation. For simplicity, we are omitting layer norms here.

An attention head indexed by \alpha computes its contribution to the residual stream by projecting the residual stream h into query, key, and value spaces: Q^{\alpha} = h W_Q^{\alpha}, \qquad K^{\alpha} = h W_K^{\alpha}, \qquad V^{\alpha} = h W_V^{\alpha}, where W_Q^{\alpha}, W_K^{\alpha}, W_V^{\alpha} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d_h}. The attention pattern is A^{\alpha} = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{Q^{\alpha} (K^{\alpha})^\top}{\sqrt{d_h}}\right), and the head writes its output back to the residual stream via \Delta h_{\alpha} = A^{\alpha} V^{\alpha} W_O^{\alpha}.

This decomposition separates information routing - information shared among token indices (via Q^{\alpha}, K^{\alpha}) from information transformation- computations that occur at specific indices (via V^{\alpha}, W_O^{\alpha}). When the attention pattern A^{\alpha} is held fixed, the contribution \Delta h_{\alpha} is linear in the residual stream.

An MLP block computes a position-wise nonlinear transformation of the residual stream: \Delta h_{\mathrm{MLP}} = W_2 \sigma(W_1 h), where \sigma is a nonlinearity applied elementwise. MLP blocks primarily transform and generate internal features, writing new directions into the residual stream.

4.5 Circuits as Computational Substructures#

One can study how subsets of a model’s components interact to implement specific computations. In transformers, such interacting subgraphs of attention heads, MLPs, features, and their connections are commonly referred to as circuits (Elhage et al. 2021).

A circuit \mathcal{C} \subseteq G is a subgraph of the model’s computational graph G, where G has:

  • Nodes: computational components (\text{Attn}_l^{(h)}, \text{MLP}_l) and features \phi_i

  • Edges: Directed connections representing the flow between computational components h_l(x)

The subgraph \mathcal{C} captures the components and features that together implement a specific computation of the model, providing mechanistic explanations to be framed at an algorithmic level while remaining grounded in the model’s representations.

The study of circuits in mechanistic interpretability has developed along two complementary lines. The first line of work, often referred to as circuit discovery (Wang et al. 2023; Conmy et al. 2023; Hanna et al. 2024; Haklay et al. 2025), aims to identify minimal sets of architectural components, such as attention heads or MLP layers, that are sufficient to reproduce a given behavior or capability of the model, such as indirect object identification (Wang et al. 2023) and numerical comparison (Hanna et al. 2023). In this setting, the capability of interest is instantiated as a dataset of queries requiring the model to have the capability to solve them, and a discovered circuit is considered successful if it recovers the model’s performance on this dataset.

The second line of work, often referred to as circuit tracing, focuses on how internal features interact and propagate across the computational graph to lead to the model decision of a particular input query (Ameisen et al. 2025; Wu and Barez 2025). Studies of this line usually make use of sparse autoencoders (detailed in Section 6) to automatically decompose mlp outputs into interpretable units, tracing attributions between these features and how the information flow converges to the final prediction.

4.6 Limitations of Foundational Works In Mechanistic Interpretability#

Mechanistic interpretability aims to provide causal explanations of model behavior, but it faces important practical and conceptual limitations.

Mechanistic explanations may be incomplete, difficult to scale to large models or complex behaviors, and sensitive to modeling assumptions. Moreover, identifying a circuit does not by itself guarantee control, robustness across models and setups, or safety (Sharkey et al. 2025).

These limitations motivate the formulation of explicit hypotheses about representation, computation, and generalization, which we examine in the next section.
