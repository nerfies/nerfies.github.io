7 Circuits in Mechanistic Interpretability#

Mechanistic interpretability aims not only to identify what information is represented inside a model, but also to explain how that information is transformed and combined to produce behavior. Feature-level analyses, such as those based on Sparse Autoencoders, provide a useful vocabulary for describing internal representations, but they are inherently local: they characterize individual directions or neurons in isolation. Many model behaviors, however, arise from structured interactions between multiple components operating together across layers. To explain such behaviors, we require a higher-level abstraction that captures patterns of computation rather than individual features. The circuit framework addresses this gap by modeling behavior as the result of coordinated subgraphs of the computation graph, making explicit how components interact causally to implement algorithms.

7.1 What Are Circuits?#

A circuit is a subgraph of the computation graph implementing a specific algorithmic behavior: \text{Circuit} = \{v_i, e_{ij}\}_{i,j \in S} \quad \text{s.t.} \quad f_\text{model}^{S}(X) \approx f_\text{model}(X), where S indexes components and f_\text{model}^{S} is the model with only those components active. X is either a specific input query or a set of queries that instantiates a specific model capability.

Circuits differ from features: features describe what information is present, while circuits describe how components interact to implement behavior.

  • Induction head circuit: Two attention heads cooperate to implement in‑context learning Olsson et al. (2022a).

  • QK circuits: Query-Key interactions prioritizing specific token positions (Kamath et al., 2025).

7.2 Circuit Discovery and Tracing Methods#

As discussed in Section 4.5, circuit discovery and tracing methods aim to identify internal mechanisms responsible for specific computations in a neural network. Below, we formalize important techniques, the SAE-based circuit discovery methods are introduced in 6.4.1 and 6.4.2.

Activation patching evaluates the causal contribution of a component or set of components S by replacing their activations with baseline values \tilde{h}_S and measuring output change: \text{Effect}(S, x) = f_\text{model}(x) - f_\text{model}(x_{[S \leftarrow \tilde{h}_S]}). This identifies which components are functionally necessary for the computation (Zhang & Nanda, 2024).

Path patching extends attribution to specific paths or edges in the computation graph. For a connection p from component i to j, one replaces activations along p with baseline values and measures output effect: \text{Effect}(p, x) = f_\text{model}(x) - f_\text{model}(x_{[p \leftarrow \tilde{h}_p]}). This allows finer-grained causal localization along information flows (Goldowsky-Dill et al., 2023).

ACDC identifies minimal sufficient sets of components S \subseteq \text{Graph}(\text{model}) that reproduce the model’s behavior. Formally, it searches for the smallest S such that f_\text{model}^{S}(x) \approx f_\text{model}(x) \quad \forall x \in \mathcal{D}, where f_\text{model}^{S} uses only components in S. Components not in S are pruned iteratively (Conmy et al., 2023).

Table 8: Circuit Discovery Methods: Trade-offs and Characteristics
Method Core Idea Strengths Limitations
Activation Patching Intervene on components; measure output change Simple, intuitive, approximates causality Confounds correlation with causation
Path Patching Patch specific information paths; measure output change Finer granularity Computationally expensive due to large number of paths; still approximate
ACDC Iteratively prune minimal sufficient paths Scales better due to automation; has been proved to recover known circuits Computationally expensive as it greedily prunes paths; misses subtle components

7.3 Circuit Discovery in Weight-Sparse Architectures#

The circuit discovery methods described above—activation patching, path patching, ACDC—are designed for dense models where circuits are hidden within fully connected computational graphs. Weight-sparse architectures (Section 6.9) fundamentally alter this landscape by making circuits structurally explicit.

Circuits visible by inspection.

In a weight-sparse transformer with <1\% active parameters, the computational graph is directly observable. To identify the circuit responsible for a specific behavior, one traces non-zero weights backward from the relevant output neurons. Because each neuron connects to only a handful of others, the resulting subgraph is sparse and human-readable without algorithmic search (Gao et al., 2025b).

This eliminates the need for iterative pruning (ACDC), gradient-based attribution, or activation patching. The circuit is the set of non-zero weights—no discovery algorithm required.

Comparison of computational costs.

Implications for verification.

Causal verification (Section 7.4) are also simplified in sparse models. Because circuits are structurally isolated, ablation experiments yield cleaner results: removing a sparse circuit removes only that circuit, without the confounding effects of distributed computation. Empirically, sparse circuits satisfy necessity and sufficiency criteria with higher precision than circuits extracted from dense models via post-hoc methods (Gao et al., 2025b; Haider et al., 2025).

Limitations.

Weight sparsity does not eliminate all challenges. Circuits may still be compositional (requiring understanding of how subcircuits interact), and the interpretability of individual neurons depends on training dynamics. Furthermore, this approach requires training sparse models from scratch or using Bridge proxies—it cannot be directly applied to existing dense models.

7.4 Causal Verification#

Once circuits are identified, two critical questions arise: are they causally responsible for the behavior, and can we modify them reliably?

Causal Necessity and Sufficiency.

A circuit is causally necessary for a behavior if removing or ablating the circuit degrades that behavior. A circuit is causally sufficient if activating the circuit alone, with all other components zeroed or corrupted, reproduces the behavior. In practice, it is hard to be both strictly necessary and sufficient—citcuits typically explain 60–80% of behavioral variance.

Evaluating Circuits and Benchmarking Discovery Methods.

A circuit is considered faithful if it is both causally necessary and causally sufficient for the behavior it is claimed to explain (Wang et al., 2023). Two widely used high-level methods for testing these properties are causal scrubbing and interchange interventions (Heimersheim & Nanda, 2024; Chan et al., 2022). Causal scrubbing evaluates necessity by replacing circuit activations with corrupted or neutralized values and measuring the resulting change in model outputs. Interchange interventions test sufficiency by swapping circuit components between clean and corrupted forward passes and assessing whether the target behavior consistently tracks the substituted circuit across inputs.

More recently, several works have proposed metrics to quantify circuit faithfulness as a continuous score (Hanna et al., 2024; Wu & Barez, 2025), allowing systematic comparisons of circuits discovered by different algorithms. By aggregating faithfulness scores across tasks and settings, benchmarks such as MIB (Mueller et al., 2025a) evaluate the performance of circuit discovery methods in identifying causally meaningful model components.

Limitations of Causal Verification.

Strict necessity and sufficiency are often unattainable. Most behaviors depend on distributed representations and multiple redundant pathways. A circuit may implement the primary mechanism (60% of variance) while other components handle edge cases. Additionally, verification assumes the circuit mechanism is faithfully captured by the identified components, but if features are entangled or nonlinear interactions are present, the measured circuit may not reflect true causal structure.

7.5 Case Study: Induction Heads#

Olsson et al. (2022a) provide a mechanistic explanation for how induction heads implement the conditional copying rule described in Definition 6.6. Consider a repeated sequence, for example

\texttt{A B C A B \underline{?}},

where the model must predict the next token following the second occurrence of B. In 2-layer attention-only models, the minimal induction circuit consists of two interacting attention heads that cooperate across layers to implement the copying computation.

The first head, often called the previous-token head, propagates information about the immediately preceding token into the residual stream. At the current position j, this head attends to the token at x_{j-1} and writes a representation of it into the residual stream. By doing so, the residual stream carries contextual information about recent tokens that downstream computations can access. The second head, the true induction head, attends from the current token x_j to all earlier positions i < j where the same token appeared (x_i = x_j). Using the context provided by the previous-token head, the induction head retrieves the residual stream representation of the token following x_i, namely x_{i+1}, and incorporates it into the residual stream at position j. This updated representation directly biases the next-token logits toward the retrieved token, effectively implementing the conditional copying rule:

(x_i = x_j) \;\Rightarrow\; \hat{x}_{j+1} \approx x_{i+1}.

Empirical evidence supports this mechanistic interpretation. Ablating either the previous-token head or the induction head significantly reduces the model’s pattern completion performance, demonstrating that both heads are causally responsible for the observed in-context learning behavior. Moreover, the circuit emerges sharply during training, coinciding with sudden improvements in in-context learning performance. In small attention-only models, this minimal two-head circuit is sufficient to reproduce the observed behavior.

In larger models containing additional layers and MLPs, the precise mechanistic trace becomes more complex, but the core principle remains the same: context is propagated across layers and then used to conditionally retrieve tokens based on repeated subsequence matches. From this perspective, the circuit view complements feature-based analyses by explicitly identifying the causal structure responsible for induction behavior and clarifying how specific heads interact across layers to implement in-context pattern completion (Olsson et al., 2022a).

7.6 A Unified Evaluation Framework for Mechanistic Interpretability#

A persistent challenge in mechanistic interpretability is the lack of standardized evaluation. Different methods—SAEs, transcoders, circuit discovery, probing—are evaluated using incompatible metrics, making it difficult to compare approaches or assess progress. This section proposes a unified evaluation framework covering five dimensions: faithfulness, completeness, stability, causal validity, and human interpretability.

7.6.1 The Need for Standardized Evaluation#

Current evaluation practices suffer from several problems:

  • Metric fragmentation: SAEs are evaluated on reconstruction loss; circuits on task performance recovery; probes on classification accuracy. These metrics are not directly comparable.

  • Cherry-picking: Results are often reported on favorable examples without systematic coverage of failure cases.

  • Missing baselines: Claims of interpretability gains rarely compare against simple baselines (e.g., random directions, PCA components, raw neurons).

  • Reproducibility gaps: Hyperparameter sensitivity and seed dependence are underreported, making replication difficult.

A unified framework enables principled comparison across methods, systematic identification of failure modes, and clearer communication of what interpretability methods actually achieve.

7.6.2 Five Evaluation Dimensions#

We propose evaluating mechanistic interpretability methods across five complementary dimensions. No single dimension suffices; strong methods should perform well across all five.

Dimension Definition Metrics Methods
Faithfulness Do identified components accurately reflect the model’s actual computation? Causal effect size; intervention consistency; counterfactual accuracy Ablation; activation patching; interchange interventions
Completeness What fraction of model behavior is explained by the identified components? Behavior coverage; reconstruction loss; unexplained variance Loss recovery; behavioral replication; residual analysis
Stability Are findings robust across seeds, checkpoints, and hyperparameters? Cross-seed agreement; checkpoint consistency; hyperparameter sensitivity Multi-seed training; checkpoint comparison; ablation studies

7.6.3 Dimension 1: Faithfulness#

Definition: Faithfulness measures whether identified components (features, circuits, explanations) accurately reflect the model’s actual internal computation, rather than post-hoc rationalizations or correlational artifacts.

Causal Effect Size: For a component C claimed to implement behavior B, measure: \text{Effect}(C, B) = \frac{|P(B | \text{do}(C = \text{active})) - P(B | \text{do}(C = \text{ablated}))|}{P(B | \text{baseline})} Higher values indicate stronger causal relationship between the component and behavior.

Intervention Consistency: Across multiple interventions on C, do effects on B remain consistent? \text{Consistency}(C, B) = 1 - \frac{\text{Var}[\text{Effect}(C, B)]}{\mathbb{E}[\text{Effect}(C, B)]^2}

Counterfactual Accuracy: When component C is patched from input x_1 to input x_2, does the model’s behavior shift as predicted? \text{CF-Acc} = \mathbb{E}_{x_1, x_2}\left[\mathbf{1}\left[f(x_1|_{C \leftarrow C(x_2)}) \approx f(x_2)\right]\right]

Evaluation protocol:

  1. Identify component C via the interpretability method being evaluated.

  2. Define target behavior B with clear operational criteria.

  3. Perform ablation: replace C with baseline (zero, mean, noise) and measure change in B.

  4. Perform activation patching: swap C between clean and corrupted inputs; verify behavior follows C.

  5. Report effect sizes with confidence intervals across multiple inputs.

Baselines: Compare against random components, PCA directions, and individual neurons to establish that identified components are more faithful than naive alternatives.

7.6.4 Dimension 2: Completeness#

Definition: Completeness measures what fraction of model behavior is explained by the identified components. High completeness means the method accounts for most of the model’s computation; low completeness means significant behavior remains unexplained.

Loss Recovery: After replacing model activations with reconstructions from identified components, what fraction of original loss is preserved? \text{LR} = 1 - \frac{\mathcal{L}_{\text{reconstructed}} - \mathcal{L}_{\text{original}}}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{random}} - \mathcal{L}_{\text{original}}} where \mathcal{L}_{\text{random}} is loss with random/ablated activations.

Behavioral Coverage: Across a diverse test suite, what fraction of behaviors \mathcal{B} can be explained by identified components? \text{Coverage} = \frac{|\{b \in \mathcal{B} : \exists C \text{ with } \text{Effect}(C, b) \geq \tau\}|}{|\mathcal{B}|}

where \tau is set to be the smallest effect that counts as meaningful.

Unexplained Variance: What fraction of activation variance is not captured by identified features? \text{UV} = \frac{\mathbb{E}[\|h - \hat{h}\|^2]}{\mathbb{E}[\|h\|^2]} where h is a model activation and \hat{h} is the reconstruction from identified components.

Evaluation protocol:

  1. Define a comprehensive behavioral test suite spanning diverse capabilities (factual recall, reasoning, safety behaviors, etc.).

  2. For each behavior, attempt to identify responsible components.

  3. Report coverage: fraction of behaviors with identified components meeting faithfulness thresholds.

  4. Report reconstruction metrics: loss recovery and unexplained variance.

  5. Analyze failure cases: which behaviors resist explanation?

Critical consideration: Methods may achieve high faithfulness on explained behaviors while leaving most behavior unexplained. Completeness ensures we track the gap between what is explained and what remains opaque.

7.6.5 Dimension 3: Stability#

Definition: Stability measures whether findings are robust to incidental choices—random seeds, training checkpoints, hyperparameter settings—that should not affect conclusions about model internals.

Cross-Seed Agreement: Train the interpretability method (e.g., SAE) with K different random seeds. Measure feature alignment: \text{Seed-Stability} = \frac{1}{K(K-1)} \sum_{i \neq j} \text{Align}(F_i, F_j) where \text{Align} measures correspondence between feature sets (e.g., via Hungarian matching on cosine similarity).

Checkpoint Consistency: For features/circuits identified at checkpoint t, do they persist at checkpoints t \pm \Delta? \text{Ckpt-Stability} = \mathbb{E}_t[\text{Align}(F_t, F_{t+\Delta})]

Hyperparameter Sensitivity: How much do results change under reasonable hyperparameter variation? \text{HP-Sensitivity} = \frac{\max_{\theta \in \Theta} \text{Metric}(\theta) - \min_{\theta \in \Theta} \text{Metric}(\theta)}{\mathbb{E}_{\theta \in \Theta}[\text{Metric}(\theta)]} Lower sensitivity indicates more robust findings.

Evaluation protocol:

  1. Train interpretability method with at least 3–5 random seeds.

  2. Compute feature/circuit alignment across seeds.

  3. Report which findings are stable (appear across all seeds) vs. unstable (seed-specific).

  4. For key claims, verify they hold under hyperparameter variation (\pm 20\% on key parameters).

  5. Distinguish “core” findings (stable) from “fragile” findings (unstable).

Reporting recommendation: Papers should report stability metrics alongside primary results. Claims based on unstable findings should be flagged as preliminary.

7.6.6 Dimension 4: Causal Validity#

Definition: Causal validity assesses whether identified components satisfy formal causal criteria of necessity (removing the component disrupts the behavior) and sufficiency (the component alone can produce the behavior).

Necessity Score: Fraction of behavior explained by the component that is lost when the component is ablated: \text{Necessity}(C, B) = \frac{P(B | \text{baseline}) - P(B | \text{do}(C = \text{ablated}))}{P(B | \text{baseline})} High necessity means the component is required for the behavior.

Sufficiency Score: Fraction of behavior that can be produced by the component alone (all other components ablated or set to baseline): \text{Sufficiency}(C, B) = \frac{P(B | \text{only } C \text{ active})}{P(B | \text{baseline})} High sufficiency means the component is enough for the behavior.

Specificity: Does the component affect only the claimed behavior, or does it have broad side effects? \text{Specificity}(C, B) = \frac{\text{Effect}(C, B)}{\sum_{B' \in \mathcal{B}} \text{Effect}(C, B')} High specificity means the component is selective for the target behavior.

Evaluation protocol (Causal Scrubbing):

  1. Necessity test: Ablate the identified circuit/feature. Measure degradation on target behavior. Compare to ablating random components of equal size.

  2. Sufficiency test: Ablate all components except the identified circuit. Measure preservation of target behavior. Compare to random circuits of equal size.

  3. Interchange test: Swap circuit activations between inputs that differ on the target behavior. Verify behavior follows the swapped circuit.

  4. Specificity test: Measure effects on a suite of non-target behaviors. Compute specificity ratio.

Benchmarks: The MIB benchmark (Mueller et al., 2025a) provides standardized tasks and metrics for causal circuit evaluation. AxBench (Wu et al., 2025) and RAVEL (Huang et al., 2024) provide feature-level causal evaluation.

7.6.7 Dimension 5: Human Interpretability#

Definition: Human interpretability measures whether explanations are actually useful to humans—whether they improve human understanding, prediction, or decision-making about model behavior.

Explanation Accuracy: Can humans predict model behavior based on the explanation? \text{Expl-Acc} = P(\text{human predicts } f(x) | \text{explanation of } f) Compare to baseline prediction accuracy without explanation.

Simulatability: After seeing explanations, can humans simulate the model’s computation on new inputs? \text{Simulatability} = \text{Corr}(\text{human prediction}, f(x_{\text{new}}))

Debugging Utility: Do explanations help humans identify and fix model errors? \text{Debug-Utility} = \frac{\text{\#Errors fixed with explanation}}{\text{\#Errors fixed without explanation}}

Consistency of Interpretation: Do different humans interpret the same explanation consistently? \text{Inter-Rater} = \kappa(\text{human}_1 \text{ labels}, \text{human}_2 \text{ labels}) where \kappa is Cohen’s kappa or a similar agreement metric.

Evaluation protocol:

  1. Prediction task: Show humans feature/circuit explanations. Ask them to predict model behavior on held-out inputs. Compare accuracy to no-explanation baseline.

  2. Identification task: Show humans model outputs with errors. With and without mechanistic explanations, ask them to identify the cause. Measure accuracy and time.

  3. Intervention task: Ask humans to propose fixes for model errors based on explanations. Measure success rate of proposed interventions.

  4. Agreement task: Have multiple raters label feature interpretations. Measure inter-rater reliability.

Considerations:

  • Human studies are expensive and introduce subjectivity. Automated proxies (LLM-based evaluation) can supplement but not replace human evaluation.

  • Expertise matters: interpretations accessible to ML researchers may be opaque to domain experts, and vice versa.

  • Task-specificity: a feature interpretable for one task may be confusing for another.

7.6.8 Integrated Evaluation Protocol#

We recommend the following integrated protocol for comprehensive evaluation:

Step 1: Define scope

  • Specify the model, layer(s), and behavior(s) under analysis.

  • Define a behavioral test suite with clear success criteria.

Step 2: Establish baselines

  • Random directions in activation space.

  • PCA components.

  • Individual neurons.

  • Supervised probes (if labels available).

Step 3: Apply interpretability method

  • Train with multiple seeds (minimum 3).

  • Record hyperparameters and training details for reproducibility.

Step 4: Evaluate across all five dimensions

  • Faithfulness: ablation and patching experiments.

  • Completeness: loss recovery and behavioral coverage.

  • Stability: cross-seed and hyperparameter sensitivity.

  • Causal validity: necessity, sufficiency, specificity tests.

  • Human interpretability: user studies or proxy metrics.

Step 5: Report comprehensively

  • Report all five dimensions, not just favorable metrics.

  • Include failure cases and unexplained behaviors.

  • Compare to baselines on all metrics.

  • Provide confidence intervals and significance tests.

7.6.10 Limitations of This Framework#

This evaluation framework has limitations that should be acknowledged:

  • Metric choice: The specific metrics proposed are not uniquely correct. Alternative formalizations may be appropriate for specific contexts.

  • Threshold arbitrariness: Proposed thresholds (e.g., necessity > 0.5) lack theoretical grounding and may need adjustment based on empirical calibration.

  • Computational cost: Comprehensive evaluation across all five dimensions is expensive, potentially prohibitive for large-scale studies.

  • Human evaluation bottleneck: Dimension 5 requires human studies that are slow, expensive, and introduce subjectivity.

  • Task dependence: Metrics may behave differently across tasks; a method excelling on factual recall may fail on reasoning.

Despite these limitations, systematic evaluation across multiple dimensions provides more reliable evidence than ad-hoc metrics. The framework should be viewed as a starting point for community standardization, not a final answer.
