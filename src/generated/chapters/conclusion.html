16 Conclusion#

Mechanistic interpretability (MI) has matured from exploratory research into an engineering discipline, with established knowledge, reproducible methods, deployed applications, and well-understood limitations. Rather than converging on a single approach, the field has crystallized into five divergent research orientations—Pragmatic, Ambitious, Developmental, Agentic, and Automated—each making rational trade-offs between completeness, cost, and applicability.

16.1 Achievements and Limits#

MI succeeds in many aspects: extracting monosemantic features via SAEs, discovering algorithmic circuits, and enabling surgical interventions. It has also helped establish AI researchers’ knowledge, mindset, and taste in other research domains. However, current methods explain only part of the model behavior, struggling with entangled computation and breaking down under distribution shift. Comprehensive analyses of frontier models remain intractable (Templeton et al., 2024).

From reverse-engineering to engineering.

Perhaps one of the most significant conceptual developments of 2024–2025 is the emergence of a third paradigm alongside post-hoc analysis and behavioral evaluation: interpretability by design. Architectural interventions (Section 6.9) demonstrate that the opacity of neural networks is not an immutable fact, but a consequence of design choices that can be revisited. Weight-sparse transformers, MOLT, and other related methods suggest a future where interpretability is a prior design constraint rather than a post-hoc recovery problem. Scaling laws (Gao et al., 2025b) have shown that large sparse models can achieve both capability and interpretability, providing empirical grounds for optimism that this paradigm may scale to frontier systems.

16.2 Unresolved Questions#

As discussed in Section 15, several fundamental open questions remain. When is mechanistic understanding actually required for safety, or do behavioral methods suffice? Can mechanisms be verified as genuine rather than post-hoc rationalizations? How do mechanisms hold up under distribution shift and adversarial pressure? These questions are not merely technical but conceptual, touching on epistemology and the definition of understanding itself.

16.3 The Road Ahead: Three Complementary Paradigms#

The field of MI is evolving from a single methodology toward three complementary paradigms:

  1. Post-hoc decomposition: The traditional approach of reverse-engineering trained models via SAEs, transcoders, circuit discovery, and causal analysis. This remains essential for understanding existing deployed systems and will likely always be necessary for models trained without interpretability constraints.

  2. Developmental analysis: Understanding how mechanisms emerge during training via singular learning theory, phase transition detection, and checkpoint analysis. This enables early intervention and prediction of capability emergence before deployment.

  3. Interpretability by design: Engineering architectures that are mechanistically transparent by construction, such as weight-sparse transformers, MOLT, KANs, and their successors. This moves interpretability upstream, showing the promise to eliminate the need for expensive post-hoc analysis.

These paradigms are not mutually exclusive. Post-hoc methods remain necessary for legacy systems and for validating architectural claims; developmental analysis informs both when to intervene during training and what architectural constraints are most effective; interpretability-by-design reduces the burden on post-hoc methods but requires validation that the architectural transparency transfers to genuine mechanistic understanding.

The maturation of MI thus lies not in the dominance of any single approach but in the principled integration of all three—matching methodology to context, validating claims across paradigms, and building cumulative understanding of how to create AI systems that are transparent, verifiable, and safe.
