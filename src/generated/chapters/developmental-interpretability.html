10 Developmental Interpretability and Training Dynamics#

Developmental Interpretability studies how specific computational structures and circuits form over the course of training and why their emergence is associated with changes in model behavior, rather than focusing only on the final trained model. By analyzing training dynamics, this line of work aims to explain phenomena such as phase transitions, capability emergence, and failure modes in mechanistic terms (Hoogland et al., 2023).

Many capabilities are not present at initialization but appear at particular stages of optimization. Tracking when these changes occur helps identify the formation of both useful and problematic behaviors. This motivates studying the learning process itself, not just the final model. Developmental Interpretability therefore seeks to monitor and analyze training trajectories to identify critical shifts in internal structure. In the long term, such insights could enable detection of emerging misaligned behaviors and inform interventions before they are fully encoded in the weights.

10.1 Grokking: Memorization-to-Generalization Phase Transitions#

Grokking is the phenomenon in which a model achieves low training loss early in training but only begins to generalize to unseen inputs after a long additional period of optimization, a pattern observed in small models trained on algorithmic tasks (Power et al., 2022; Nanda et al., 2023).

A canonical setting is modular addition, where training accuracy becomes perfect early, while test accuracy remains low for many training steps before undergoing a sharp increase (Power et al., 2022; Nanda et al., 2023).

Mechanistic analyses of these models show that, during this extended plateau, internal representations and attention patterns change gradually rather than remaining static (Nanda et al., 2023). In particular, they identify progress measures that track the slow emergence of algorithmic structure inside the network before generalization appears. When these internally measured structures become sufficiently strong, test performance improves rapidly, producing the observed phase transition (Nanda et al., 2023).

Takeaway: In these controlled settings, grokking corresponds to gradual internal changes during training that manifest externally as a sudden transition in generalization.

10.2 Training Dynamics and the Emergence of Internal Mechanisms#

Recent work analyzes training dynamics to understand how qualitative changes in model behavior arise. One study focuses on factual knowledge acquisition in language models and shows that training proceeds through distinct phases: an initial phase dominated by learning coarse statistical regularities, followed by an extended plateau with little factual recall, and a later phase in which factual knowledge begins to emerge (Zucchet et al., 2025a). Mechanistically, this plateau coincides with the formation of attention-based circuits that support recall. The authors further analyze how data distribution and curriculum choices shape these dynamics and study the interaction between factual learning and hallucinations, highlighting systematic patterns in when correct and incorrect responses emerge during training. Complementary work focuses on the emergence of sparse attention mechanisms and their connection to abrupt improvements in performance (Zucchet et al., 2025b). This study shows that sparse attention can arise suddenly during training and that its emergence depends on properties of the training data, such as repetition. By combining theoretical analysis with empirical results, the authors link changes in attention structure to non-linear learning dynamics.

10.3 Singular Learning Theory and the Local Learning Coefficient#

Singular Learning Theory (SLT) provides a complementary theoretical lens by relating properties of the loss landscape, such as degeneracy and singularities, to the emergence of internal structure (Hoogland et al., 2023). Traditional statistical learning theory often assumes that model parameters are identifiable and that the loss landscape behaves regularly near optima. In modern neural networks, however, the mapping from parameters to functions is often many-to-one, meaning that multiple parameter configurations can implement the same function. This property creates degeneracies in the loss landscape: directions in parameter space where the loss changes very little. Singular Learning Theory (SLT) provides a framework for studying such models and reasoning about the consequences of these degenerate regions (Watanabe, 2009).

For a model with parameters \mathbf{w} at a local minimum \mathbf{w}^*, the Local Learning Coefficient (LLC), denoted \lambda(\mathbf{w}^*), is a positive number that measures the amount of degeneracy in the loss landscape near \mathbf{w}^*. In other words, it captures how many ways the parameters can vary locally without changing the loss value (Watanabe, 2009; Lau et al., 2023).

Smaller LLC values indicate high degeneracy: many parameter configurations near the minimum achieve the same loss. Larger LLC values indicate low degeneracy: the local region is tightly constrained.

Formally, the LLC is defined via the volume-scaling rate of the loss landscape near a minimum. This makes it a more general measure of “flatness” than simple Hessian-based methods, because it captures higher-order contributions to local geometry. Empirical estimators can track the LLC throughout training, providing a way to observe developmental changes in model structure (Lau et al., 2023; Hoogland et al., 2025).

Empirical studies have used the LLC to monitor training in transformer models and other architectures. Researchers have observed that transitions and critical points in the LLC often coincide with changes in internal network structure and functional behavior. Tracking these changes provides a quantitative way to study the emergence of structure during training without relying only on final performance metrics (Hoogland et al., 2025).

10.4 Stagewise Influence and Developmental Data Attribution#

Most traditional training data attribution methods measure the impact of a training example only on the final model parameters. However, neural networks often learn in stages, with distinct phases where capabilities or structural features emerge. The influence of individual training examples can therefore change dramatically during training, including sudden increases, decreases, or even sign reversals (Lee et al., 2025).

Lee et al. (2025) propose a stagewise data attribution framework that extends classical influence methods to track the impact of training points throughout the learning process. Their approach is grounded in Singular Learning Theory (SLT), which characterizes neural networks as singular models—statistical models where the Fisher information matrix is degenerate and multiple distinct parameter configurations yield equivalent predictions. Unlike regular models where the Bayesian posterior concentrates smoothly around a unique maximum likelihood estimate, singular models exhibit non-smooth, stagewise development.

Classical influence functions rely on inverting the Hessian of the loss landscape, which becomes ill-defined when the Fisher information matrix is degenerate. For singular models like deep neural networks, this motivates an alternative formulation. The framework employs the Bayesian Influence Function (BIF):

For a training dataset D, training point z_i, and quantity of interest \phi(\theta) (e.g., loss on another data point), the Bayesian Influence Function is defined as: \text{BIF}(z_i, \phi) = -\text{Cov}_{p(\theta|D)}[\ell(z_i; \theta), \phi(\theta)] where \ell(z_i; \theta) is the loss on data point z_i at parameters \theta, and the covariance is computed over the posterior distribution p(\theta|D).

Unlike classical influence functions that require Hessian inversion, the BIF measures influence through covariance estimation over the local posterior distribution. This distributional approach is estimated via stochastic-gradient MCMC sampling, enabling scalable computation without explicit Hessian access while naturally capturing the geometry of singular loss landscapes.

The core prediction is that influence is not static but exhibits non-monotonic dynamics that peak at developmental transitions. Lee et al. (2025) validate this prediction analytically and empirically in a hierarchical feature-learning model (Saxe et al., 2019). In this controlled setting, influence undergoes sign flips as the model transitions between learning different levels of the semantic hierarchy (animals versus plants, then mammals versus birds, then dogs versus cats), with sharp peaks occurring precisely when each new hierarchical distinction emerges. These predictions are further confirmed through leave-one-out retraining experiments that directly measure the effect of removing individual training examples at different stages of training.

At scale, Lee et al. (2025) demonstrate these phenomena in Pythia language models (Biderman et al., 2023b) trained on the Pile dataset (Gao et al., 2020). Token-level influence exhibits non-monotonic changes, including sudden peaks and sign reversals that align with known developmental milestones such as the formation of induction heads. For tokens with structural roles (e.g., delimiters, tokens participating in induction patterns), influence undergoes dramatic shifts at specific training checkpoints corresponding to circuit formation events identified in prior mechanistic interpretability work (Olsson et al., 2022b).

10.5 Deceptive Alignment and Training Dynamics#

A critical concern is that deceptive or hidden objectives can emerge during training even when loss metrics appear normal, driven by complex circuit formation that evades behavioral detection (Hubinger et al., 2024). Models may learn goals orthogonal to the training objective, exhibiting behavioral invisibility where emergent misaligned behaviors go undetected in standard performance metrics. Mechanistic analysis can reveal circuit-level evidence of deceptive reasoning patterns—internal computations that contradict stated outputs. This underscores a fundamental limitation of behavioral safety measures: loss curves and benchmark performance provide incomplete information about what algorithms the model actually implements. Consequently, monitoring training dynamics and tracing circuit formation is essential for detecting early signs of deception before problematic objectives crystallize in the weights (Hubinger et al., 2024).

10.6 Case Study: Induction Heads#

Singh et al. (2024) study induction heads from a training-dynamics perspective, focusing on how such structures emerge during optimization. While earlier work showed that induction heads appear abruptly and coincide with a sharp improvement in in-context learning (Olsson et al., 2022a), this work seeks to explain the mechanisms behind this phase transition.

The authors train transformers on controlled synthetic sequence tasks and introduce an optogenetics-inspired causal intervention framework that allows intermediate activations to be clamped or manipulated throughout training, enabling causal analysis of how specific components influence learning dynamics.

They find that induction behavior does not arise from a single head suddenly activating. Instead, many attention heads across layers gradually develop induction-like behavior, and their contributions to in-context learning are largely additive. Although each component evolves smoothly, their combined effect produces a sharp, macroscopic transition in loss and accuracy, explaining why induction heads appear to “switch on” abruptly.

Using systematic clamping experiments, the authors further identify three interacting subcircuits whose joint dynamics give rise to induction behavior.

Finally, they show that the timing and strength of induction head emergence depend on dataset statistics and task structure, indicating that induction heads arise from interactions between data, optimization, and model structure rather than being inevitable architectural artifacts.
