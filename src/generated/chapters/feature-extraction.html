6 Feature Extraction and Multi-Layer Analysis#

The goal of feature extraction is to identify features that are distributed across many neurons but correspond to coherent semantic or computational units. There are two problems. First, polysemanticity: individual neurons encode multiple semantics. Second, scale: modern models contain millions to billions of neurons. We can overcome these problems by decomposing activations into monosemantic features using sparse reconstruction. One version of this approach is sparse autoencoders (SAEs) (Olshausen & Field, 1996; Le, 2013), which provide a concrete way of extracting interpretable features from model activations (Cunningham et al., 2023).

This section gives an overview of how SAE techniques recover interpretable features and a practical overview of SAE variants. It gives advice on when to use the different variations. Then, multi-layer methods such as transcoders and crosscoders are introduced and compared to SAE methods and a wide variety of alternatives.

6.1 Sparse Autoencoders#

Sparse Autoencoders are a way of extracting monosemantic features from polysemantic neurons. At a high level, they are trained to replicate the dynamics of a single MLP or attention block with each direction representing a distinct concept (Figure 4). They are optimised for accuracy to the real MLP or attention block and for having few active features for any given forward pass.

A sparse autoencoder learns an encoder–decoder pair E : \mathbb{R}^d \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{sparse}}},\; D : \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{sparse}}} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^d such that activations h \in \mathbb{R}^d are reconstructed via \hat{h} = D(E(h)), while encouraging sparse feature activations E(h). It helps localize feature activations .

Standard Formulation.

Given activations h \sim \mathcal{H}, the standard SAE objective is \mathcal{L}_{\text{SAE}} = \mathbb{E}_{h \sim \mathcal{H}} \left[ \| h - D (z) \|_2^2 + \lambda \| z \|_1 \right], \quad z = E(h), where z \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{sparse}}} denotes feature activations and \lambda > 0 controls sparsity (Cunningham et al., 2023).

Why SAEs Work.

SAEs are motivated by the hypothesis that features occur sparsely in the data and are linearly recoverable from a model’s hidden states. Under sparsity assumptions, overcomplete dictionaries can recover monosemantic directions (features) even when raw neurons are highly polysemantic (Cunningham et al., 2023; Bricken et al., 2023).

From Monosemanticity to Concept Identification.

While sparsity and overcompleteness provide the basis for discovering monosemantic features, practical interpretability requires identifying the semantic concepts to which these features correspond in natural languages. Empirically, SAE features exhibit selective activation to structured token patterns that recur across input sequences, suggesting sensitivity to specific abstract semantic or functional concepts. Automated analysis pipelines (Gur-Arieh et al., 2025a; Paulo et al., 2025) automatically aggregate highly activating inputs to infer the latent concept captured by each feature (see Section 13.7 for a detailed discussion).

Training Dynamics and Evaluation.

Training of SAEs is sensitive to initialization, sparsity strength, and decoder normalization. Without proper constraints and hyperparameters, the features may collapse, duplicate, or absorb one another (see Appendix 19 for hyperparameters and stability guidance). SAEs are typically evaluated for reconstruction loss (fidelity of activation reconstruction), feature absorption (degree to which features remain distinct), and downstream testing loss recovery (whether replacing activations with reconstructions preserves model behavior). Together, these metrics assess both the quality of the decomposition and its mechanistic relevance to the computation of the original model.

6.2 SAE Variants and Architectural Improvements#

There are several variants of the standard SAE. One key reason to use alternatives is that the standard SAE training penalizes activation magnitude, which can lead to low activation even when a feature is clearly present (known as the shrinkage effect). Different optimisation frameworks also have different tunable parameters. Which one to use depends on the analysis goal, target layer type, size of the model, and budget constraints.

Gated SAEs introduce multiplicative gates to decouple feature selection from magnitude: z_i = \underbrace{\mathbbm{1} \left( (W_{\text{gate}} h_\ell + b_{\text{gate}})_i > 0 \right)}_{\text{Gating Path}} \odot \underbrace{\text{ReLU}(W_{\text{in}} h_\ell + b_{\text{in}})_i}_{\text{Magnitude Path}} where h_\ell \in \mathbb{R}^d is the activation at layer \ell, i indexes the i-th feature, W_{\text{in}} \in \mathbb{R}^{{d_{\text{sparse}}} \times d} and b_{\text{in}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{sparse}}} are encoder E parameters, W_{\text{gate}} \in \mathbb{R}^{{d_{\text{sparse}}} \times d} and b_{\text{gate}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{sparse}}} are gate’s parameters. This mitigates shrinkage effects induced by the \ell_1 penalty (Rajamanoharan et al., 2024a).

JumpReLU SAEs learn per-feature activation thresholds. Given encoder pre-activations a = W_{\text{in}} h_\ell + b_{\text{in}}, feature activations are: z_i = \begin{cases} a_i & \text{if } a_i > \theta_i \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} where \theta_i is a learned threshold parameter. A smooth surrogate is used during training to enable gradient flow Rajamanoharan et al. (2024b).

Top-k SAEs (Gao et al., 2025a) impose an explicit hard sparsity constraint. Let a = W_{\text{in}} h_\ell + b_{\text{in}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{sparse}}} denote encoder pre-activations. Top-k SAEs define the feature activations as: z_i = \begin{cases} a_i & \text{if } i \in \mathcal{I}_k(a) \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} where \mathcal{I}_k(a) indexes the k largest entries of a. This formulation explicitly enforces the constraint |z|_0 \le k, providing a more direct and interpretable feature sparsity constraint than L1 regularization. Unlike L1 loss, it does not suppress activation magnitudes, thereby avoiding the feature shrinkage issue observed in L1-based SAE training (Gao et al., 2025a).

Batch Top-k SAEs enforce sparsity at the batch level. Given a batch of pre-activations A = [a^{(1)}, \ldots, a^{(B)}] \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{sparse}} \times B}, a subset \mathcal{J} \subset \{1,\ldots,d_{\text{sparse}}\} of k features is selected as the batch-wide top-k: \mathcal{J} = \arg\max_{|\mathcal{J}|=k} \sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}} \sum_{b=1}^B |a_j^{(b)}| Activations are then set as z_j^{(b)} = \begin{cases} a_j^{(b)}, & j \in \mathcal{J} \\ 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} for each example b. This enforces global feature competition, reduces variance in feature usage across the batch, and prevents collapse (Bussmann et al., 2024).  

SAE variants differ fundamentally in how they enforce sparsity. No single variant is optimal across all regimes.

6.3 Practical Guidance: Selecting SAE Variants#

Practitioners face a decision problem: which SAE variant should be used for a given model, layer, and analysis goal? This section provides practical heuristics synthesized from empirical findings and architectural considerations. These recommendations are provisional—the field lacks comprehensive benchmarks comparing variants across all settings—but offer a starting point for informed decisions.

In the following, we consider four factors that primarily determine which SAE variant is appropriate, and present the decision tree based on these factors.

  1. Analysis goal: Exploratory discovery vs. targeted investigation vs. deployment/monitoring

  2. Layer type: Residual stream vs. MLP output vs. attention output

  3. Model scale: Small (<1B) vs. medium (1-10B) vs. large (>10B parameters)

  4. Resource constraints: Compute budget, hyperparameter tuning capacity, tolerance for training instability

Step 1: What is your primary goal?

  • Exploratory discovery (understand what features exist) \rightarrow Go to Step 2

  • Targeted investigation (find features for specific behavior) \rightarrow Consider supervised methods first (DAS, probes); if unsupervised needed, go to Step 2

  • Deployment/monitoring (real-time feature detection) \rightarrow Prioritize inference speed; Top-K or standard SAE with small dictionary

Step 2: What layer type are you analyzing?

  • Residual stream \rightarrow Go to Step 3

  • MLP outputs \rightarrow Consider Transcoders (predict post-MLP from pre-MLP); if single-layer SAE needed, go to Step 3

  • Attention outputs \rightarrow Standard SAE or Gated SAE; attention-specific variants are less developed

  • Cross-layer analysis \rightarrow Crosscoders or CLTs (if compute permits)

Step 3: What are your resource constraints?

  • Limited compute / need fast iteration \rightarrow Top-K SAE (no \lambda tuning; fixed sparsity)

  • Moderate compute / some tuning capacity \rightarrow Gated SAE (best reconstruction-sparsity tradeoff in most settings)

  • Ample compute / extensive tuning possible \rightarrow JumpReLU SAE (learned thresholds; strong performance but sensitive)

Step 4: What model scale?

  • Small models (<1B) \rightarrow Any variant works; use this regime to validate methodology before scaling

  • Medium models (1-10B) \rightarrow Gated or Top-K recommended; JumpReLU if tuning budget available

  • Large models (>10B) \rightarrow Top-K or Batch Top-K (most stable at scale); Gated SAE if reconstruction quality critical

6.3.2 Common Pitfalls and How to Avoid Them#

Pitfall 1: Using standard \ell_1 SAE without considering alternatives

  • Standard SAEs suffer from shrinkage bias; Gated SAEs solve this with minimal added complexity.

  • Recommendation: Use Gated SAE as the default unless you have specific reasons for standard.

Pitfall 2: Over-tuning \lambda instead of switching to Top-K

  • If you’re spending significant time tuning the sparsity coefficient, Top-K eliminates this hyperparameter entirely.

  • Recommendation: If \lambda tuning takes more than a few runs, switch to Top-K with target sparsity.

Pitfall 3: Using the same expansion factor everywhere

  • Early layers may need smaller dictionaries (2-4x); late layers may need larger (8-16x).

  • Recommendation: Start with 4x expansion, then adjust based on dead feature rate and reconstruction quality.

Pitfall 4: Ignoring layer type differences

  • SAEs on residual stream vs. MLP outputs vs. attention outputs behave differently.

  • Recommendation: For MLP analysis, use transcoders; for cross-layer analysis, use crosscoders or CLTs.

Pitfall 5: Not checking for dead features

  • High dead feature rates (>20%) indicate wasted capacity and possible training issues.

  • Recommendation: Monitor dead feature rate; use neuron resampling or switch to Batch Top-K if problematic.

6.4 Multi-Layer Feature Analysis: Transcoders and Crosscoders#

Standard SAEs analyze single layers in isolation. However, features evolve as information flows through the network. Transcoders and Crosscoders enable analysis across layers and token positions, revealing how representations transform through nonlinearities.

6.4.1 Transcoders: Bridging Nonlinear Layers#

A transcoder learns a sparse mapping between the input and output to an MLP layer: h_{\ell}^{out} \approx W_{\text{out}} z + b_{\text{out}}, \quad z = \sigma(W_{\text{in}} h_\ell^{in} + b_{\text{in}}) where z \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{sparse}}} are sparse feature activations bridging the nonlinearity between layers \ell and \ell+1, W_{\text{out}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times {d_{\text{sparse}}}} and b_{\text{out}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d} are decoder D parameters. The transcoder reconstructs post-nonlinearity activations using features extracted from pre-nonlinearity activations (Dunefsky et al., 2024).

Architecture and Motivation.

Standard SAEs struggle to explain activations after nonlinear transformations (MLP layers, attention). A transcoder applies sparse decomposition directly to the nonlinear mapping, learning features that explain the transformation. Training minimizes: \mathcal{L} = \|h_{\ell}^{out} - (W_{\text{out}} z + b_{\text{out}})\|_2^2 + \lambda \|z\|_1 where z = \sigma(W_{\text{in}} h_\ell + b_{\text{in}}).

When Transcoders Are Necessary.

Single-layer SAEs often suffice for residual-stream analysis, where features largely propagate linearly. However, MLP blocks apply nonlinear transformations that distort feature space. Transcoders are essential when analyzing MLPs, understanding feature interactions, or tracking how concepts evolve across layers (Dunefsky et al., 2024).

6.4.2 Cross-Layer Transcoders#

A Cross-Layer Transcoder (CLT) is a sparse feature decomposition spanning all layers of a neural network. Each feature \mathbf{a}^{\ell} reads from the residual stream at layer \ell via: \mathbf{z}^{\ell} = \text{JumpReLU}(W_{\text{in}}^{\ell} h_\ell) and each feature can write to the MLP outputs of all subsequent layers via layer-specific decoder matrices: \hat{\mathbf{y}}^{\ell'} = \sum_{\ell=1}^{\ell'} W_{\text{out}}^{\ell \to \ell'} \mathbf{z}^{\ell} where \hat{\mathbf{y}}^{\ell'} reconstructs the original MLP output at layer \ell'.

CLTs are trained jointly across all layers to minimize: \mathcal{L} = \sum_{\ell=1}^L \|\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{\ell} - \mathbf{y}^{\ell}\|_2^2 + \lambda \sum_{\ell=1}^L \sum_{i} \tanh(c \cdot \|W_{\text{out},i}^{\ell}\| \cdot z_i^{\ell}) where c is the sharpness hyperparameter (Ameisen et al., 2025).

Replacement Models and Attribution Graphs.

A key insight is that CLTs enable the construction of a replacement model: a version of the original model where all MLP layers are replaced by their CLT reconstructions. Running this replacement model involves:

  • Computing CLT feature activations \mathbf{z}^{\ell} at each layer \ell

  • Replacing MLP outputs \mathbf{y}^{\ell} with reconstructions \hat{\mathbf{y}}^{\ell} from features in layers 1 through \ell

  • Keeping attention layers unchanged

Empirically, CLT replacement models match the original model’s most-likely token on approximately 50% of prompts in initial experiments on Gemma-2-2B (Ameisen et al., 2025). However, this figure should be interpreted with important caveats (see below). When analyzing a specific prompt, a local replacement model freezes attention patterns and normalization statistics from the original model’s forward pass, then adds layer-specific error corrections. Under these conditions, the local replacement model can exactly match the original model’s computation on that prompt while decomposing it into interpretable CLT features.

The claim that CLT replacement models achieve \sim50% most-likely token match requires careful qualification:

Conditions for reported fidelity:

  • Results reported primarily on Gemma-2-2B; generalization to other architectures (e.g., Llama, GPT variants) and larger scales (70B+) remains underexplored.

  • Fidelity was measured on evaluation prompts drawn from small sequences drawn from the CLT training data. Performance under significant distribution shift (e.g., out-of-domain tasks, adversarial inputs) is not well characterized.

  • The 50% figure reflects top-1 token agreement; softer metrics (e.g., top-5 agreement, KL divergence) may show different patterns.

Conditions for exact local matching:

  • Attention patterns must be frozen from the original forward pass, the CLT does not model or reconstruct attention computations.

  • Layer normalization statistics (means, variances) must be cached from the original model; the replacement model does not recompute these.

  • Error correction terms are added per-layer to compensate for reconstruction residuals; without these, exact matching fails.

Factors affecting fidelity:

  • Model scale: Preliminary evidence suggests fidelity improves with model size, possibly because larger models exhibit more structured, less entangled representations (Ameisen et al., 2025). However, systematic scaling studies are limited.

  • Layer depth: Middle layers often show higher reconstruction fidelity than early or final layers, where representations may be more input-specific or output-constrained respectively.

  • Domain: Fidelity on factual recall and syntactic tasks appears higher than on complex reasoning or arithmetic, where MLP computations may be more nonlinear and less amenable to sparse linear approximation.

These caveats do not invalidate CLTs as a tool for mechanistic analysis, but practitioners should verify fidelity on their specific model, layer, and task before drawing strong conclusions from CLT-based circuit analysis.

Why CLTs Enable Better Circuit Analysis.

Unlike per-layer transcoders, CLTs reveal direct feature-to-feature interactions across layers: a feature in layer \ell directly influences features in later layers through its contribution to their inputs. This simplifies circuit structure—features need not re-encode information at each layer. Additionally, by replacing MLPs with CLT reconstructions, the remaining computation becomes approximately linear (with frozen attention patterns and normalization), enabling principled attribution: each feature’s activation can be expressed as the sum of its input edges up to its activation threshold (Ameisen et al., 2025).

However, the degree of linearization achieved depends on several assumptions:

  • Attention freezing: By caching attention patterns, the replacement model treats attention as a fixed routing operation rather than a dynamic, input-dependent computation. This simplifies analysis but means CLT-based circuits describe computation given specific attention patterns, not the attention computation itself.

  • Normalization caching: Freezing LayerNorm statistics removes a source of nonlinearity but introduces dependence on the specific input used to cache these statistics.

  • Sparse linear approximation: The core assumption that MLP outputs are well-approximated by sparse linear combinations of features may break down for computations involving complex feature interactions or highly nonlinear transformations.

When these assumptions hold, CLTs provide a powerful lens for circuit discovery. When they fail, particularly for complex reasoning or out-of-distribution inputs, CLT-based explanations may be incomplete or misleading.

Computational Cost and Practical Considerations.

A CLT associates each transformer layer with an encoder \mathbb{R}^d \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{ed} and every ordered layer pair (\ell_s < \ell_t) with a decoder \mathbb{R}^{ed} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^d. The total parameter count scales as N_{\text{CLT}}(L,d,e) = \tfrac{e d^2}{2}\left(L^2 + L\right), highlighting quadratic growth in both model depth and width, as shown in Figure 6.

Training a CLT requires significant upfront cost, which scales with the number of features and layers. For reference, training the CLT suite for Gemma-2-9B required approximately 3,844 H100 GPU-hours at 50% FLOPs efficiency (Ameisen et al., 2025). This cost is amortized over many circuit analyses on the trained CLT. The resulting interpretability gains (cross-layer features, lower interference, better attribution) justify the investment for systematic mechanistic analysis.

For these reasons, besides Anthropic’s foundational work (Ameisen et al., 2025; Lindsey et al., 2025c), CLT applications remain limited: a “greater-than” mechanism study (Merullo et al., 2025), an open-source extension (Lindsey et al., 2025a), and analysis of multilingual representations using CLTs (Harrasse et al., 2025a). This scarcity of downstream applications in the current literature likely reflects the considerable challenges in training CLTs at scale.

Open Questions for CLT Research.

Several important questions remain open:

  • How does CLT fidelity scale in large models?

  • Can CLTs be extended to jointly model attention and MLP computations?

  • What evaluation protocols can reliably detect when CLT-based explanations are unfaithful to the original model’s computation?

  • How sensitive are CLT circuits to the choice of training data distribution?

6.4.3 Crosscoders: Analyzing Cross-Layer Features#

A crosscoder learns sparse features by reading activations from multiple layers simultaneously: \hat{x}_{\ell, t} = W_{\text{out}} z_{\ell,t} + b_{\text{out}}, \quad z_{\ell,t} = \text{ReLU}(W_{\text{in}} [h_{\ell',t}]_{\ell' \in L} + b_{\text{in}}) where z_{\ell,t} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{sparse}}} encodes sparse features constructed from activations across a set of layers L at token position t. Crosscoders resolve cross-layer superposition by tracking features that persist through the residual stream (Lindsey et al., 2024).

Crosscoder is trained by minimizing: \mathcal{L} = \sum_{\ell\in L} \|\hat{x}^{\ell} - h^{\ell}\|_2^2 + \lambda \sum_{\ell\in L} \sum_{i} \|W_{\text{out},i}^{\ell}\| \cdot z_i^{\ell} where \lambda is the hyperparameter.

Causal vs. Acausal Crosscoders.

Crosscoders support two information flow patterns:

  • Causal Crosscoders: Read only from layers \ell' \leq \ell at the current position. This respects the forward pass causality: earlier layers cannot read from later layers.

  • Acausal Crosscoders: Read from any layer \ell' regardless of depth ordering. Useful for understanding post-hoc feature relationships across the full model.

Applications to Circuit Analysis.

By extracting cross-layer features, crosscoders enable understanding how features propagate through the residual stream across layers, removing duplicate feature representations that complicate circuit analysis. This simplification allows features to “jump” across intervening identity operations, substantially reducing circuit complexity. More importantly, crosscoders reveal how input-relevant features transform as they flow through the network, distinguishing which features persist unchanged versus which are actively modified by downstream computation (Lindsey et al., 2024).

6.5 Comparing SAEs, Transcoders, and Crosscoders.#

Single-layer SAEs are efficient and locally interpretable but miss cross-layer feature evolution. Transcoders improve this by imitating the behavior of the MLP, revealing nonlinear transformations, though they require sequential analysis across depth. Cross-layer transcoders span multiple layers for global information flow but are extremely expensive. Crosscoders take a different approach, extracting shared features across layers simultaneously to resolve cross-layer superposition and simplify circuits without chaining predictions.

6.6 Case Study: Induction Heads#

Induction heads are attention heads that implement a conditional copying rule over repeated subsequences. Let (x_1,\dots,x_T) be a token sequence and consider positions i < j < T such that x_i = x_j. An attention head at layer \ell and head h is an induction head if, when predicting the token at position j+1, its attention weights satisfy \alpha_{j \rightarrow i+1}^{(\ell,h)} \gg \alpha_{j \rightarrow k}^{(\ell,h)} \quad \text{for most } k \neq i+1, and its value projection causes the residual stream at position j to contribute logits favoring x_{i+1}.

Equivalently, induction heads approximate the function (x_i = x_j) \;\Rightarrow\; \hat{x}_{j+1} \approx x_{i+1}, enabling in-context prediction by copying continuations following earlier matching prefixes. Induction heads were first identified and analyzed by Olsson et al. (2022a).

Consider the sequence: \texttt{A B C A B \underline{?}} After observing the repeated prefix A B, an induction head attends from the second B to the position following the first B, producing a strong prediction for C. This copying behavior generalizes across tokens and positions and does not require explicit supervision.

While induction heads were originally characterized as specific attention heads implementing this copying rule, subsequent work has shown that the underlying computation is more naturally understood at the level of distributed representations rather than isolated architectural components.

Feature extraction methods, such as SAEs, can decompose attention layer outputs into sparse, interpretable features that capture meaningful activation patterns beyond individual attention weights (Kissane et al., 2024). Modern analyses suggest that induction heads are not isolated mechanisms but participate in distributed feature circuits, where multiple interacting features recur and compose across layers (Dunefsky et al., 2024; Singh et al., 2024; Kissane et al., 2024; Kamath et al., 2025).

SAEs trained on model activations reveal families of features that correspond to recurring patterns and contextual dependencies in the residual stream, some of which align with behaviors traditionally attributed to induction heads (Kissane et al., 2024). These features can be analyzed to understand how models maintain and manipulate information across tokens in context.

Cross-layer analyses, including transcoders, indicate that interpretable features can persist across multiple layers, suggesting that certain computations are represented consistently rather than being rediscovered at each layer (Dunefsky et al., 2024; Kamath et al., 2025). This perspective highlights that attention heads, including those exhibiting induction-like behavior, may form part of broader, distributed feature compositions when examined through modern decomposition methods.

6.7 Challenges and Failure Modes of SAEs#

Sparse Autoencoders have become a standard tool for feature extraction, but they exhibit systematic failure modes that can compromise interpretability claims. A careful understanding of these issues is therefore essential when using SAEs as mechanistic tools. This section provides a critical and systematic treatment of these challenges, including comparisons with alternative decomposition methods.

6.7.1 Dead Features#

A dead feature is a dictionary element that never activates, or activates only extremely rarely, across the training distribution. Dead features represent wasted capacity and can constitute between 10–50% of the dictionary in poorly tuned SAEs (Bricken et al., 2023). Several factors can lead to the emergence of dead features. First, inappropriate initialization can place features in low-density regions of activation space, preventing them from receiving meaningful gradient signals. Second, an excessively strong sparsity penalty \lambda may suppress feature activations early in training, stopping features from discovering a useful role. Finally, competitive dynamics between features can exacerbate this issue: once a subset of features captures common patterns, the remaining features may be starved of signal and fail to activate.

Dead features can be detected by tracking feature activation frequencies during training; features with activation rates below 0.01% are effectively inactive. Several mitigation strategies have been proposed, including neuron resampling, in which dead features are periodically reinitialized toward examples with high reconstruction error (Bricken et al., 2023). Another effective approach is to reduce the sparsity penalty during early training via a warmup schedule and increase it gradually. More recent Batch Top-K variants explicitly enforce minimum feature usage across batches, which substantially reduces the prevalence of dead features (Bussmann et al., 2024).

6.7.2 Feature Duplication#

Feature duplication occurs when multiple dictionary elements encode the same or highly overlapping concepts (Templeton et al., 2024). Although this increases the apparent dictionary size, it provides redundant rather than distinct features. Duplication may arise when sparsity pressure is insufficient, allowing multiple features to co-activate on similar inputs, from optimization dynamics favoring local minima with duplicated features, or from imbalances in the training data. Duplication can be detected using pairwise cosine similarity between decoder columns, where high similarity (e.g., >0.8) indicates potential redundancy. Co-activation statistics provide complementary evidence, as duplicated features tend to activate together (Korznikov et al., 2025).

6.7.3 Feature Absorption and Splitting#

Another class of failure modes concerns feature granularity. Feature absorption occurs when a single feature captures multiple distinct, but related, concepts, reducing representational specificity (Chanin et al., 2025). For example, a single “code” feature may activate for Python, JavaScript, and SQL instead of learning language-specific representations. Conversely, feature splitting occurs when one coherent concept is unnecessarily divided across several features (e.g., “dog at sentence start” vs. “dog after article”). These issues can be identified through activation patterns. Absorbed features tend to activate frequently across diverse inputs, whereas split features often share similar semantic labels but differ in context and show high mutual information. Both degrade interpretability: absorbed features blur multiple computations, while split features distribute a single mechanism across several units.

6.7.4 Feature Instability Across Seeds#

SAE features are also sensitive to random initialization, optimizer stochasticity, and data ordering. Training the same architecture with different random seeds can therefore produce substantially different dictionaries (Paulo & Belrose, 2025). In practice, frequent and semantically clear features tend to be stable, while rare or context-specific features vary considerably. Exact feature alignment across seeds is often below 50%, although broader semantic clusters may remain consistent. This instability limits the reliability of mechanistic claims based on individual features. Ensembling across seeds can improve robustness but increases computational cost. For this reason, feature stability across seeds should be reported alongside reconstruction loss when evaluating SAEs.

6.7.5 Identifiability and Theoretical Concerns#

A fundamental question for SAE-based interpretability is whether the learned features are identifiable, that is, whether training reliably recovers the “true” underlying features (if such features exist) or merely finds one of many equally valid decompositions (Klindt et al., 2025).

Given activations h generated by some unknown sparse process h = Df^* + \epsilon where f^* are ground-truth features, does minimizing the SAE objective recover f^* (up to permutation and scaling)?

In classical Dictionary Learning, identifiability is guaranteed under conditions of low coherence, meaning that dictionary elements are sufficiently orthogonal to one another. When coherence is high, however, ambiguity arises: multiple distinct sparse combinations of features from the same dictionary can generate the same data point (Garfinkle & Hillar, 2016; Schnass & Vandergheynst, 2008). In the context of large language model activations, high coherence is likely unavoidable due to the strong internal structure and correlations within the model’s feature space (Li et al., 2025c). This makes identifiability particularly challenging and provides a theoretical explanation for several empirical observations about Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs).

Recent work has raised concerns about the reliability of the features learned by SAEs (Mueller et al., 2025b). When trained on identical data but with different random seeds, SAEs tend to produce substantially different feature sets (Paulo & Belrose, 2025), suggesting a lack of uniqueness in the learned representations. Moreover, studies have shown that SAE features can often be reconstructed from smaller sets of more basic features using a meta-SAE (e.g., representing “Einstein” as a combination of “Germany,” “Starts with E,” “Space and Galaxies,” “Science,” and “Prominent Figures”), indicating that these features are not atomic (Leask et al., 2025). Finally, phenomena such as feature splitting and feature absorption further illustrate how SAEs may merge or decompose simpler features in order to optimize sparsity, rather than recover a unique underlying basis (Chanin et al., 2025). Taken together, these behaviors are consistent with the failure of identifiability under high-coherence conditions and help explain why SAE features can be unstable, non-atomic, and seed-dependent.

Recent progress in finetuning language models around a fixed sparse autoencoder offers a potential path forward. In this setting, model activations are explicitly constrained to be sparse combinations of decoder features while largely preserving task performance (Chen et al., 2025). In particular, recent work aimed at limiting superposition fine-tunes the language model jointly with the SAE while enforcing orthogonality constraints on the decoder matrix (Miller et al., 2026). By explicitly satisfying low-coherence conditions, this approach restores identifiability: there exists a unique representation of activations in terms of features, and these features can be reliably recovered.

Relation to Causal Relevance.

Identifiability concerns connect directly to causal relevance: even if an SAE feature is interpretable and reconstructs activations well, it may not correspond to a causally meaningful unit of computation.

Diagnostic criteria:

  • Necessity: Does ablating the feature degrade task performance? (Low necessity suggests the feature is not causally central.)

  • Sufficiency: Does activating only this feature (and related features) reproduce the behavior? (Low sufficiency suggests the feature captures correlation, not causation.)

  • Specificity: Does the feature’s causal effect match its semantic interpretation? (A “sentiment” feature that causally affects syntax but not sentiment is misidentified.)

These causal tests should be standard practice when claiming that SAE features represent meaningful computational units.

6.7.6 Practical Recommendations#

Given these challenges, we recommend the following practices for SAE-based interpretability:

  1. Report failure mode metrics: Alongside reconstruction loss, report dead feature percentage, duplication rate (via cosine similarity), and stability across seeds.

  2. Validate causal relevance: Do not assume interpretable features are causally meaningful. Test necessity and sufficiency through targeted interventions (e.g. feature ablation or activation steering) and measure downstream effects.

  3. Compare methods: For safety-critical applications, compare SAE features against supervised baselines (DAS, probes) to assess whether SAEs recover the relevant structure.

  4. Use multiple SAE variants: Different variants (Gated, Top-K, JumpReLU) impose different inductive biases. Convergent findings across variants provide stronger evidence.

  5. Acknowledge identifiability limits: Frame SAE features as “one valid decomposition” rather than “the true features.” Multiple decompositions may be equally valid for reconstruction while differing in the set of features recovered.

  6. Consider alternatives for targeted analysis: When specific concepts are the focus, supervised methods (e.g. probes) may be more reliable than hoping SAEs discover the relevant features.

6.8 Comparison with Alternative Feature Extraction Methods#

Sparse Autoencoders are one approach for extracting features from neural network activations. Alternative methods drawn from signal processing and machine learning make different modeling assumptions and therefore offer distinct trade-offs in terms of interpretability, scalability, and robustness.

6.8.1 Non-Negative Matrix Factorization (NMF)#

Non-Negative Matrix Factorization decomposes activations as H \approx WA, where both the dictionary W and the activation matrix A are constrained to be non-negative. This constraint induces a natural parts-based interpretation in which features combine additively without cancellation. As a result, NMF often yields components that are easier to interpret than unconstrained linear decompositions. In addition, closed-form update rules exist for certain NMF objectives, which can mitigate some optimization pathologies. Under specific conditions such as separability, NMF also enjoys stronger uniqueness guarantees than unconstrained sparse coding (Donoho & Stodden, 2003).

Despite these advantages, NMF is not always well suited to neural network activations. Activations produced by modern architectures, particularly after LayerNorm or nonlinearities such as GELU, are not naturally non-negative, requiring preprocessing steps that may distort representational structure. Moreover, NMF does not explicitly encourage sparsity unless additional regularization is introduced, and scaling NMF to large dictionaries is computationally challenging. Using an NMF inspired approach, Yun et al. (2021) factorizes residual-stream activations with non-negativity constraints, producing additive components that are easier to interpret and visualize across layers. However, for feature extraction and causal analyses, empirical work has largely focused on Semi-NMF which relaxes the non-negativity constraint on W. Shafran et al. (2025) showed that Semi-NMF can recover features competitive with SAEs on small-scale evaluations, but systematic comparisons at large scale remain limited.

6.8.2 Principle Component Analysis (PCA)#

Principal Component Analysis decomposes activations by finding an orthonormal basis of directions that maximise explained variance. Given a mean-centered activation matrix H \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}, PCA yields a low-rank approximation H \approx U \Sigma V^T, where the columns of V correspond to principal directions ordered by explained variance. Fitting PCA is fast, stable and scalable. Yet, PCA introduces constraints and inductive biases that can be misaligned with neural representations. PCA identifies a single set of global directions that maximises variance across the full activation distribution, which can mix multiple sources of variation when the relevant structure is region or context-dependent. In addition, PCA enforces orthogonality between components, a constraint that may not reflect how features are organized in practice, where factors can be correlated or partially overlapping. Its misalignment is further reflected in recent benchmarks. In Wu et al. (2025), PCA underperforms most baselines, including SAEs, on both steering and concept-detection evaluations. While in some localization settings, PCA outperform SAEs (Mueller et al., 2025a), it still lags behind simpler supervised baselines.

6.8.3 Independent Component Analysis (ICA)#

Independent Component Analysis seeks to decompose activations as H = WS, where the components of S are statistically independent (Hyvärinen & Oja, 2000). Independence provides a principled objective grounded in information theory, and under well-specified generative assumptions, specifically, linear mixtures of independent sources, ICA can provably recover the underlying components. Unlike SAEs, ICA does not require tuning a sparsity hyperparameter.

However, ICA makes strong assumptions that are often violated in neural representations. Classical ICA assumes a complete setting in which the number of sources equals the dimensionality of the representation, and extensions are required for overcomplete decompositions. Neural network features may also fail to satisfy statistical independence, limiting ICA’s applicability. Although ICA has been applied to neural network activations (Hyvärinen et al., 2023), it has not matched the interpretability gains reported for SAEs in recent large-scale studies, possibly because neural features tend to be sparse rather than independent.

6.8.4 Supervised Feature Extraction Methods#

When the concepts of interest are known in advance, supervised methods can directly optimize representations aligned with those targets. Examples include Distributed Alignment Search (DAS), which uses supervision to learn a change of basis that aligns representations with high-level features (Geiger et al., 2024); Supervised Multi-Dimensional Scaling (SMDS), which projects activations to preserve concept-level similarities (Tiblias et al., 2025); and Differential Binary Masking (DBM), which learns binary masks that select relevant activation dimensions for specific concepts (De Cao et al., 2020).

When labelled data are available, supervised approaches often outperform SAEs on targeted concept recovery tasks (Huang et al., 2024; Chaudhary & Geiger, 2024). They also avoid the need to choose dictionary sizes or sparsity penalties, since complexity is determined by the concept set itself. However, these methods require labelled data for each concept and therefore cannot discover previously unknown features. They do not scale naturally to open-ended exploration across entire models, and each concept typically requires separate training. As a result, supervised methods are best suited to settings where specific, predefined concepts are of interest, such as safety-critical behaviors like toxicity or deception, whereas SAEs remain preferable for exploratory analysis.

6.8.5 Dictionary Learning Baselines#

Classical dictionary learning methods provide useful baselines for evaluating SAEs. Techniques such as K-SVD alternate between sparse coding and dictionary updates and are known to converge under certain conditions (Aharon et al., 2006). Comparative studies suggest that the primary advantage of SAEs lies in their learned encoder, which enables fast inference, rather than in fundamentally superior dictionary quality (Cunningham et al., 2023). As such, classical dictionary learning remains an important reference point for assessing the added value of SAE architectures.

6.9 Architectural Interventions for Intrinsic Interpretability#

The methods surveyed thus far, SAEs, Transcoders, Crosscoders, share a common assumption: the model architecture is fixed, and interpretability must be recovered post-hoc. A parallel research program, which we term Architectural Interventions for Interpretability, inverts this assumption by asking whether models can be designed to be mechanistically interpretable by construction (Pearce et al., 2025; Park et al., 2025; Oldfield et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2025; Dooms et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2025b; Adler et al., 2025; Draye et al., 2025).

This approach is motivated by the inherent limitations of post-hoc decomposition. Standard Transformer architectures are optimized without interpretability constraints, incentivizing superposition as an efficient compression strategy. Post-hoc methods like SAEs attempt to undo this compression, but they introduce reconstruction gaps, require substantial compute, and may fail to capture the full causal dynamics of the original model. Architectural interventions aim to solve the interpretability problem upstream, creating systems where circuits are visible by design rather than laboriously extracted.

6.9.1 Weight Sparsity via L_0 Regularization#

The most significant recent advancement is the Weight-Sparse Transformer, introduced by Gao et al. (Gao et al., 2025b). This approach treats sparsity not as a tool for inference efficiency but as a tool for circuit discovery.

Mechanism.

The model is trained to minimize a composite objective: \begin{equation*} \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{task}} + \lambda \cdot \|\theta\|_0 \tag{2} \end{equation*} where \mathcal{L}_{\text{task}} is the standard cross-entropy loss and \|\theta\|_0 counts non-zero parameters. Since the L_0 norm is non-differentiable, training employs stochastic gates drawn from a Hard-Concrete distribution, allowing gradients to flow while penalizing the probability of each gate being open. At inference, gates are discretized, yielding networks where the vast majority of connections are exactly zero.

Interpretability gains.

Under high sparsity regimes (e.g., <1\% active weights), the computational graph simplifies dramatically. A neuron receiving input from only 5–10 others (rather than thousands) cannot implement the complex interference patterns required for polysemanticity. Consequently, neurons tend to align with single, interpretable concepts—monosemanticity emerges by structural necessity rather than post-hoc extraction.

Circuits can be identified by simply visualizing non-zero weights connected to a target output, eliminating the need for activation patching or ACDC. Validation via mean ablation shows that these sparse circuits satisfy necessity and sufficiency criteria with higher precision than circuits identified in dense models.

The capability-interpretability frontier.

A critical contribution is formalizing the trade-off between performance and interpretability. As the sparsity penalty \lambda increases, interpretability improves but task loss degrades, defining a Pareto frontier. Crucially, Gao et al. (2025b) discover a scaling law: increasing model size shifts this frontier outward. Large sparse models can achieve both better capability and better interpretability than small dense models, suggesting that over-parameterization coupled with sparsity constraints may be the path toward interpretable frontier models.

6.9.2 Sparse Attention Post-Training: SPARTAN#

The dense attention matrix implies every token attends to every other token, creating a quadratic web of connections difficult to audit. Sparse Attention Post-Training (SPARTAN) applies L_1 regularization to the attention patterns while finetuning with a constrained optimization objective to maintain the same cross-entropy performance  (Draye et al., 2025)  (Lei et al., 2025).

The result: diffuse “soft” attention patterns collapse into “hard” sparse patterns. A head that previously attended broadly across context snaps to attending only to specific operands required for the current computation. This reveals that much attention connectivity is redundant noise, and SPARTAN effectively performs circuit discovery by pruning the attention graph. Using Cross-Layer Transcoders, they show that the reduced number of mediating components renders attention attribution tractable. This illustrates the potential of sparse attention in the context of attribution graphs, as it enables a unified view of features and circuits.

6.9.3 Activation-Level Interventions: SoLU and Its Limitations#

An earlier approach targeted the activation function rather than connectivity. Softmax Linear Units (SoLU) replace element-wise activations with a spatial softmax (Elhage et al., 2022b): \begin{equation*} x \mapsto x \cdot \text{softmax}(x) \tag{3} \end{equation*} This forces neurons within a layer to compete: if one neuron fires strongly, it suppresses its neighbors through lateral inhibition.

Results and the hydraulic effect.

SoLU successfully increased the fraction of monosemantic neurons identifiable by human inspection. However, it revealed a critical limitation: the hydraulic effect. When prevented from using neurons for superposition, models simply displaced the compression elsewhere—into the residual stream, into sub-threshold activations, or into other components not subject to the constraint. This indicates that local architectural constraints are insufficient when global incentives for compression remain. It underscores the need for either global constraints (like weight sparsity applied throughout the network) or fundamentally different computational primitives.

6.9.4 Computational Primitive Modifications: MOLT#

One alternative approach to learning interpretable structure is to rethink what atomic computational units to learn (Oldfield et al., 2025; Park et al., 2025; Braun et al., 2025; Bushnaq et al., 2025). One recent example of this is the so-called Sparse Mixtures of Linear Transforms (MOLT), representing a conceptual shift from feature extraction to transformation extraction (Lindsey et al., 2025b).

Motivation.

Standard SAEs and Transcoders assume computation consists of detecting features. However, for algorithmic tasks (arithmetic, geometric reasoning), models perform operations—“add 3,” “rotate vector.” An SAE attempts to represent these as massive lookup tables of states, which obscures the underlying mechanism.

Mechanism.

MOLT replaces the MLP with a sparse mixture of linear transformation matrices. Each “neuron” is not a vector but a matrix that can be dynamically applied to the residual stream. For modular arithmetic, a MOLT might learn a single rotation matrix implementing addition, rather than memorizing thousands of input-output pairs.

This aligns the architecture with the true algorithmic structure of the task, yielding higher mechanistic faithfulness than standard feature-based decompositions for tasks involving systematic transformations.

6.9.5 Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks#

Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (KANs) represent the most radical departure from standard architectures (Liu et al., 2025b). Based on the Kolmogorov-Arnold representation theorem, KANs place learnable activation functions (B-splines) on edges rather than fixed activations on nodes.

This inversion makes KANs explicitly compositional. Because non-linearities are univariate and visible on edges, researchers can perform symbolic regression on learned splines to recover exact mathematical formulas—for instance, rediscovering physics equations from data. While currently slower to train and unproven at LLM scale, KANs represent the ambitious ideal of a model where weights are interpretable mathematical functions.

6.9.6 The Bridge Methodology: Interpretable Proxies for Dense Models#

Retraining frontier models with sparsity constraints is computationally prohibitive. Gao et al. propose the “Bridge” methodology as a practical alternative (Gao et al., 2025b).

A Bridge is a weight-sparse model trained to mimic the internal representations of a pretrained dense model—minimizing reconstruction error of layer-wise activations rather than training on the original task. The sparse model serves as an interpretable proxy: if it achieves high fidelity and is mechanistically interpretable, researchers can analyze its circuits to understand the dense model’s behavior.

Preliminary evidence suggests that interventions on sparse proxies (e.g., ablating identified circuits) can be mapped back to induce similar behavioral changes in dense models. However, validating proxy fidelity—ensuring the proxy captures the same mechanisms rather than behaviorally equivalent simulations—remains an open problem (see Section 15).
