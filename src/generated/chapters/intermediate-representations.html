8 Understanding Intermediate Computation: Logit Lens and Probing#

Between feature extraction (what the fundamental components of features are) and circuit analysis (how components interact), there lies a gap: understanding what computation happens at intermediate layers. This section covers methods that analyze hidden layer activations to reveal how information transforms as it flows through the network.

8.1 Logit Lens: Tracing Predictions Through Depth#

Logit lens (nostalgebraist, 2020) applies the model’s unembedding matrix to activations at intermediate layers, revealing what the model would predict if computation stopped at that layer. For a hidden activation h_\ell at layer \ell, logit lens computes: \text{logits}_\ell = W_U h_\ell where W_U represents the model’s unembedding matrix. This reveals the trajectory of predictions across layers, showing when and where the model commits to specific outputs (nostalgebraist, 2020).

Logit lens provides intuitive insight into the model’s decision-making process across depth. Early layers typically show diverse possible predictions with high entropy, while later layers progressively narrow and refine them toward the final output. By examining this trajectory, researchers can identify at which layer critical decisions emerge: whether the model commits early to a prediction or progressively refines it layer-by-layer.

For example, in a 12-layer language model processing the sequence “A B C A B”, the logit lens may reveal that the token “C” emerges as the top-20 candidates at layer 6. Subsequent layers then progressively amplify this signal, eventually promoting it to the top-ranked token at the final layer. Conversely, for more ambiguous induction tasks involving complex logic, early and middle layers may exhibit low confidence across a broad distribution of tokens, with the model only converging to a specific prediction in the last few layers.

Limitations of Vanilla Logit Lens.

The vanilla logit lens has critical limitations. Applying the unembedding matrix to intermediate activations assumes that the hidden state is in a format the output layer expects, but intermediate representations may reside in vector spaces different from final activations. The unembedding matrix is only trained to operate on the final residual stream after all transformations; applying it to layer-6 activations extracts relevant information but may not precisely reflect the model’s underlying computation at that layer (nostalgebraist, 2020).

8.2 Tuned Lens: Learning Per-Layer Transform#

Tuned lens (Belrose et al., 2025) addresses the vector space mismatch problem of logit lens by learning per-layer affine transformations that map intermediate activations to the output layer’s expected space: \text{logits}_\ell^{\text{TL}} = W_U (W_\ell h_\ell + b_\ell), where W_\ell and b_\ell are learned affine transformations for layer \ell. These transforms are trained to minimize the divergence between the tuned lens’ predicted distribution p_\ell^{\text{TL}} = \operatorname{softmax}(\text{logits}_\ell^{\text{TL}}) and the final model output distribution p_{\text{final}}, typically via \min_{W_\ell,b_\ell}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\big[\,\mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{final}}(x)\;\|\;p_\ell^{\text{TL}}(x))\big].

Tuned lens improves on basic logit lens by correcting for the fact that different layers operate in different representational spaces. Rather than naively applying the final output layer, tuned lens learns what transformation is needed at each layer to make intermediate activations speak the same language as the final layer (Belrose et al., 2025).

The trade-off is that tuned lens requires training additional parameters (one affine transform per layer), but this is computationally cheap compared to other mechanistic methods.

8.3 Case Study: Refusal Vectors and Sleeper Agents#

A refusal vector is a direction in a model’s residual stream activations that corresponds to the model declining to produce undesirable or harmful content. Let h_t^\ell \in \mathbb{R}^d denote the residual stream at layer \ell and token position t. We define a refusal vector \mathbf{r}^\ell \in \mathbb{R}^d at layer \ell as: \mathbf{r}^\ell = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}_\text{harmful}} [h_t^\ell(x)] - \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}_\text{harmless}} [h_t^\ell(x)], where \mathcal{D}_\text{harmful} contains prompts that should be refused and \mathcal{D}_\text{harmless} contains neutral or safe prompts.

Projecting h_t^\ell onto \mathbf{r}^\ell quantifies the strength of the refusal signal at layer \ell: s_t^\ell = \langle h_t^\ell, \mathbf{r}^\ell \rangle.

A large positive s_t^\ell correlates with refusal behavior, and ablating or modifying this component can reduce the model’s tendency to refuse. Refusal vectors can be used to interpret, steer, or probe models along safety-relevant directions in residual space.

Building on Definition 8.3, we can use logit lens to examine how refusal vectors manifest across layers and why the same approach is less effective for latent behaviors like sleeper agents in Definiton 2.

For a refusal vector \mathbf{r}^\ell defined at layer \ell, logit lens projects intermediate hidden states h_t^\ell to the output vocabulary via the unembedding matrix W_U: \hat{y}_t^{(\ell)} = W_U\, h_t^\ell. The resulting layer-wise logits \hat{y}_t^{(\ell)} approximate what the model would predict if layer \ell were the final layer. When a refusal vector contributes significantly to the activations, the logits associated with refusal behavior may become noticeable at intermediate layers, providing a tentative trace of how the model encodes refusal. Projections along \mathbf{r}^\ell can be used to quantify the strength of this signal: s_t^\ell = \langle h_t^\ell, \mathbf{r}^\ell \rangle.

Sleeper agents encode a conditional, latent objective that activates only under a hidden trigger (e.g., year = 2024). For such behavior, the difference \Delta \hat{y}_t^{(\ell)} = \hat{y}_t^{(\ell)}(\text{trigger}) - \hat{y}_t^{(\ell)}(\text{non-trigger}) may appear gradually across layers as the model increasingly favors tokens associated with the latent objective. In this sense, logit lens can still reveal contributions to potentially harmful tokens and track how these emerge through the network, similarly to refusal vectors.

8.4 Patchscopes: Leveraging the Language Model Itself to Explain Intermediate Representations#

Patchscopes (Ghandeharioun et al., 2024) leverages the expressive capacity of large language models to translate internal representations into natural-language explanations. Let h^l_t denote a hidden representation at layer l and token position t. Patchscopes produces a textual explanation of h^l_t, denoted by e(h^l_t), via e(h^l_t) = f_{\text{model}}(p_{[h^{l^*}_{t^*} \leftarrow h^l_t]}), where l^* and t^* specify the layer and token position to which the representation is patched, and p is an elicitation prompt designed to encourage the language model f_{\text{model}} to decode the patched representation into text that expresses the contained information.

For example, consider the prompt illustrated in the Figure 1 of Ghandeharioun et al. (2024) p = \text{``cat$\rightarrow$cat; 135$\rightarrow$135; hello$\rightarrow$hello; ?''}, where t^* corresponds to the position of the token “?”. The notation [h^{l^*}_{t^*} \leftarrow h^l_t] indicates that, during the forward pass of f_{\text{model}} on p, the hidden representation at layer l^* and position t^* is replaced with h^l_t. Under this construction, information encoded in h^l_t can be elicited through the model’s prediction for the next token at the “?” position.

Because the mapping from intermediate representations to natural language may be too complex to be captured by an affine transformation, Patchscopes extends the paradigm of the logit lens and tuned lens by delegating the representation-to-text transformation to the language model itself1. In this view, the language model acts as an activation decoder.

The decoding process proceeds by patching the representation of interest into a designated token position within an elicitation prompt. As the model processes the prompt, the semantic information embedded in the patched representation can be surfaced in the model’s generated output. Crucially, this approach relies on the model’s own learned and generalized text-generation capabilities to perform the transformation from internal activations to natural language.

Limitations of Patchscopes.

A key limitation of Patchscopes is that injecting an external representation into an elicitation prompt can shift the model’s internal activations into an OOD regime. This is expected as the decoder is a standard pretrained language model that has not been explicitly trained for representation-to-text decoding. Although the model may still successfully surface salient information from the patched representation, the decoding process can be degraded, and critical aspects of the original representation may be distorted or lost.

  1. A concurrent work, SelfIE (Chen et al., 2024b), explores a similar direction. While we focus on Patchscopes as a reference framework, both methods share closely related motivations and methodologies.↩︎

8.5 LatentQA: Finetuning the Language Model as Representation Decoder#

LatentQA (Pan et al., 2024) formulates representation decoding as a supervised learning problem via Latent Interpretation Tuning (LIT), which fine-tunes an activation decoder to perform representation-to-text decoding. The core step of LIT is to construct a finetuning dataset D consisting of samples (h^l_t, q, a), where h^l_t is a cached hidden representation from a target model, and (q, a) is a question–answer pair that queries the information encoded in h^l_t.

Specifically, h^l_t denotes the activation at layer l and token position t when the target model processes a “control prompt” designed to embed a specific attribute or concept into the representation. An example control prompt from Figure 3 of Pan et al. (2024) is: \text{``In your next reply, please try to evoke a brief sense of triumphant hubris.''} If we define h^l_t as the activation at layer l corresponding to the final token (“.”) of this prompt, the resulting representation is expected to encode the instruction to respond with triumphant hubris. In this case, a corresponding question–answer pair may be: q = \text{``In what way does the assistant craft its responses to the user?''} a = \text{``The assistant crafts responses that briefly highlight a sense of self-assured victory.''} Both the control prompt and the (q, a) pair are generated by prompting an off-the-shelf proprietary language model (Pan et al., 2024).

LatentQA addresses the OOD issue inherent in Patchscopes-style methods by explicitly finetuning the activation decoder on the representation-to-text decoding task. Through LIT, the decoder is trained to directly ingest and process raw activation vectors. Ideally, after being exposed to a sufficiently diverse collection of question–answer pairs over many different representations, the decoder learns which aspects of the activation to attend to and how to verbalize them conditioned on the query.

Limitations of LatentQA.

The central limitation of LatentQA lies in the generalizability of the learned representation-to-text decoder. When the finetuning dataset D is insufficiently large or diverse, the decoder may overfit by memorizing superficial correspondences between representations and textual descriptions, rather than learning the underlying algorithmic transformation. Recent efforts, such as Predictive Concept Decoders (PCDs) (Huang et al., 2025) and Activation Oracles (AOs) (Karvonen et al., 2025), mitigate this issue by constructing more diverse finetuning datasets to expand the decoder’s coverage and robustness.

8.6 Probing Classifiers: Capabilities, Critiques, and Causal Limitations#

Probing classifiers are among the most widely used tools for understanding neural network representations. A probe trains a classifier—typically linear—to predict a property (e.g., part-of-speech, sentiment, entity type) from model activations. High probe accuracy is interpreted as evidence that the property is “encoded” in the representation. However, the relationship between probe accuracy and mechanistic understanding is subtle and contested. This section reviews probing methodology, its critiques, and its appropriate role in interpretability research.

8.6.1 Standard Probing Methodology#

A probing classifier is a supervised model trained to predict a target property y from neural network activations h_\ell at layer \ell: \hat{y} = g(W_{\text{probe}} h_\ell + b_{\text{probe}}) where g is an activation function (identity for regression, softmax for classification), and (W_{\text{probe}}, b_{\text{probe}}) are learned parameters. The probe is trained on labeled examples (h_\ell^{(i)}, y^{(i)}) to minimize prediction error.

Linear probes constrain g to be linear, testing whether the property is linearly accessible. MLP probes allow nonlinear g, testing whether the property is present in any recoverable form.

What Probing Can Show.

Probing provides evidence about information content: whether activations contain recoverable information about a property. High probe accuracy indicates the information is present; low accuracy indicates it is absent or deeply encoded.

What Probing Cannot Show.

Probing does not establish causal relevance: whether the model actually uses that information for its predictions. A representation may encode information that is never accessed by downstream computation—a “leftover” from earlier processing or a byproduct of representing correlated features.

8.6.2 The Probe Causality Problem#

High probe accuracy does not imply causal relevance. The model may encode information without using it, or the probe may detect correlates of the target property rather than the property itself (Belinkov, 2022; Hewitt & Liang, 2019).

Failure modes:

  • Correlational encoding: The probe detects features correlated with the target (e.g., word frequency correlates with part-of-speech) rather than the target itself.

  • Unused information: Information may be encoded but never read by subsequent layers. The model’s actual computation may rely on different features.

  • Probe expressivity: A sufficiently powerful probe can extract information that requires complex computation to access, information the model itself may not use.

This problem is fundamental: probing measures information presence, not information use. Bridging this gap requires causal methods.

8.6.3 Control Tasks and Selectivity#

A major critique of probing is that high accuracy may reflect probe capacity rather than representation quality. Hewitt & Liang (2019) introduced control tasks to address this concern.

A control task assigns random labels to inputs, breaking any genuine relationship between activations and the target property. A probe trained on the control task measures the baseline extractability of random patterns from the representation.

Selectivity is defined as: \text{Selectivity} = \text{Acc}_{\text{linguistic}} - \text{Acc}_{\text{control}} where \text{Acc}_{\text{linguistic}} is probe accuracy on the true task and \text{Acc}_{\text{control}} is accuracy on the control task.

High selectivity indicates the probe succeeds because of genuine encoding, not because the representation is easily separable by any linear classifier. Low selectivity suggests the probe may be exploiting representation geometry rather than task-relevant structure.

Implications of Control Tasks.

Control task experiments have revealed that many probing results showing “encoding” of linguistic properties suffer from low selectivity—random labels are nearly as extractable (Hewitt & Liang, 2019). However, selectivity improves with model depth and size, suggesting more task-specific encoding in larger models, though this varies by property: syntactic properties typically exhibit higher selectivity than semantic ones. This fragmented picture underscores why probing studies must report control task accuracy alongside primary results; claims about encoding are only meaningful when selectivity is substantially positive.

8.6.4 Probe Complexity vs. Causal Relevance#

A related critique concerns the relationship between probe complexity and causal relevance. If a property can only be extracted by a complex (nonlinear, high-capacity) probe, does this indicate the property is “deeply encoded” or merely that the probe is doing substantial computation itself?

Observation: More complex probes achieve higher accuracy on most tasks.

Interpretation problem: Does high accuracy with a complex probe indicate:

  1. The property is encoded but in a complex, nonlinear form?

  2. The probe itself is computing the property from simpler features?

If (b), the probe demonstrates that the property is computable from the representation, not that it is encoded in the representation in any meaningful sense.

Minimum Description Length Probes.

To address this, Voita & Titov (2020) proposed evaluating probes via the Minimum Description Length (MDL) principle: prefer probes that achieve good accuracy with minimal complexity. The MDL framework jointly considers data cost (how well the probe compresses labels given representations) and model cost (how complex the probe itself is). A property is considered “more encoded” if it can be extracted with a simpler probe, shifting the question from “can we extract it?” to “how easily can we extract it?”

Practical Guidance.

When interpreting probing results, prefer linear probes when possible—if linear probes fail but MLP probes succeed, interpret with caution. Crucially, report probe architecture and capacity alongside accuracy, and compare against probes trained on random representations to establish baselines. For quantitative comparison, consider MDL-based evaluation, which provides a principled way to trade off accuracy against model complexity.

8.6.5 Amnesic Probing and Causal Probing#

Early work demonstrated that individual neurons could be manipulated to control morphological properties such as gender, number, and tense (Bau et al., 2019), motivating more systematic, direction-based approaches. The idea of intervening on representations via probes was then formalized progressively: Giulianelli et al. (2018) first demonstrated that probes trained on intermediate activations could steer model behavior, and Ravfogel et al. (2020) extended this via Iterative Nullspace Projection (INLP), which repeatedly projects out linearly encoded information. Building on this lineage, Elazar et al. (2021) introduced amnesic probing: after identifying a direction encoding property P, project it out of the representation and measure downstream task degradation.

To bridge the gap between information presence and causal relevance, several methods attempt to make probing causal.

Amnesic Probing.

Given a probe direction w encoding property P:

  1. Compute the projection: h' = h - \frac{h \cdot w}{\|w\|^2} w

  2. Run the model with h' replacing h

  3. Measure change in downstream task performance

If performance degrades, the information was causally used. If performance is unchanged, the information was encoded but unused.

Limitations: Amnesic probing assumes the property is encoded in a single linear direction. If encoding is distributed or nonlinear, projection may not fully remove the information, leading to false negatives (concluding information is unused when it is).

Causal Probing via Interchange Interventions.

A stronger causal test swaps the probed representation between inputs that differ on the target property:

  • Input x_1 has property P = p_1; input x_2 has property P = p_2.

  • Replace the representation component encoding P in x_1 with that from x_2.

  • If the model’s behavior shifts to reflect p_2, the encoding is causally active.

This provides stronger evidence than amnesic probing but requires identifying the specific representation component, not just demonstrating its existence.

8.6.6 Critiques of Attribution and Saliency Methods#

Probing is often used alongside attribution methods (saliency maps, attention visualization, gradient-based attribution) to understand model behavior. However, attribution methods face their own critiques that affect how probing results should be interpreted.

Faithfulness of Attributions.

Attribution methods aim to identify which inputs or components most influenced an output. However, Jacovi & Goldberg (2020) distinguish between plausibility (do attributions seem reasonable to humans?) and faithfulness (do attributions reflect actual model computation?).

Key findings on attribution faithfulness

: Gradient-based saliency can highlight features the model is insensitive to (Adebayo et al., 2018b), attention weights correlate weakly with causal importance (Jain & Wallace, 2019; Wiegreffe & Pinter, 2019), and different attribution methods often disagree substantially on the same inputs (Krishna et al., 2024). Collectively, these findings suggest that attribution methods are fundamentally unreliable guides to what information a model actually uses.

Implications for Probing.

This unreliability propagates into probing studies. Probes trained on attention patterns may not reveal causally important information since attention itself may not be faithful. Similarly, using gradients to identify which features to probe can bias toward high-magnitude gradients that lack causal importance. When probing and attribution methods disagree, resolving the disagreement requires causal interventions rather than passive observation.

8.6.7 Circuit Fragility and Robustness Concerns#

A related concern applies to circuit-level findings from mechanistic interpretability: identified circuits may be fragile, failing to generalize across distributions or model variants.

Evidence for Circuit Fragility.

Circuits identified on one task may not transfer to related tasks, even when the underlying computation seems similar (McGrath et al., 2023). Small changes to model weights through finetuning or continued pretraining can disrupt identified circuits while preserving task performance, suggesting redundancy or alternative pathways. Moreover, circuits identified in small models may not have direct analogues in larger models (Olsson et al., 2022a), limiting the transferability of findings across model scales.

Implications for Probing.

Circuit fragility reveals a gap in probing’s explanatory power: stable probe accuracy across checkpoints does not guarantee stable circuit implementation, and information encoded at layer \ell may be read by different circuits under different conditions. This suggests that probing findings—which identify what information is encoded—may not reliably predict how that information is used. Complementing probing with circuit analysis is therefore necessary to understand not just encoding but routing.

8.6.8 When is Probing Appropriate, and When is it Not?#

Given these critiques, we provide guidance on appropriate uses of probing.

Table 12: Appropriate and Inappropriate Uses of Probing
Appropriate Uses Inappropriate Uses
Establishing information presence (“is P encoded?”) Claiming causal relevance (“the model uses P”)
Comparing encoding strength across layers Concluding a property is “not used” from low probe accuracy
Auditing for encoded sensitive information Claiming mechanistic understanding from probes alone
Generating hypotheses for causal investigation Interpreting probe weights as model weights
Early detection / triage before expensive analysis Evaluating alignment or safety from probe results

Probing as Hypothesis Generation.

The most productive framing treats probing as hypothesis generation: probes identify candidate properties that may be causally relevant, which must then be tested via causal methods (ablation, patching, circuit analysis). This positions probing as an efficient first step, not a final answer.

8.6.9 Best Practices for Probing Studies#

Based on the critiques above, we recommend the following practices:

  1. Report control task baselines: Always include selectivity metrics.

  2. Use minimal probe complexity: Prefer linear probes; justify MLP probes when necessary.

  3. Include random baselines: Compare to probes on shuffled activations.

  4. Test causal relevance: Complement probing with amnesic probing or interchange interventions.

  5. Report layer-wise patterns: Show how encoding varies across layers, not just peak accuracy.

  6. Acknowledge limitations: Explicitly state that probing shows information presence, not causal use.

  7. Cross-validate: Report variance across random seeds and data splits.

  8. Consider alternatives: For targeted properties, supervised methods (DAS, DBM) may provide more reliable results than probing followed by post-hoc causal testing.

Despite the critiques above, probing remains valuable for specific applications. The Simple probes can catch sleeper agents research (MacDiarmid et al., 2024) demonstrates that simple linear probes trained on residual stream activations can detect latent backdoor behavior in sleeper agent models even when the trigger and the harmful behavior are unknown.

In a sleeper agent model f_\theta, the model behaves normally for one context (e.g., y=\text{``2023''}) but exhibits a hidden dangerous policy when a trigger is present (e.g., y=\text{``2024''}) (MacDiarmid et al., 2024).

To detect this latent behavior, one can construct a linear defection detector by computing a detector direction \mathbf{d}^\ell at a given residual layer \ell. Let h_t^\ell(x) denote residual activations at the final token position. Given a small labeled fit dataset of prompts, the detector direction is defined as: \mathbf{d}^\ell = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}_\text{true}}[h_t^\ell(x)] - \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}_\text{false}}[h_t^\ell(x)], where \mathcal{D}_\text{true} and \mathcal{D}_\text{false} are contrast pairs that correlate with the latent policy.

Projecting test activations onto this direction yields a scalar detector activation: s_t^\ell = \langle h_t^\ell(x),\, \mathbf{d}^\ell \rangle, which can then be used as a classifier score to distinguish inputs likely to activate the sleeper agent from those that are not. Empirically, this approach achieves high accuracy in identifying the latent objective.

Why this works despite the critiques: In this application, the goal is detection (information presence), not mechanistic understanding. The probe identifies that deceptive intent is encoded, which is sufficient for a safety filter. Whether the model “uses” this encoding in a mechanistically clean way is secondary to the practical goal of flagging dangerous inputs.

This illustrates that probing remains valuable when the goal is auditing or detection, while its limitations apply most strongly to mechanistic claims.
