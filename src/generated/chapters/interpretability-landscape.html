3 The Interpretability Landscape#

The previous sections established that behavioral evaluation alone is insufficient for safety guarantees, and that mechanistic analysis—direct study and manipulation of model internals—offers a path toward causal understanding. But mechanistic methods are computationally intensive and do not scale easily. This raises a practical question: When is mechanistic analysis necessary, and when do simpler methods suffice?

This section examines the major families of interpretability methods, their trade-offs, and the conditions under which each is appropriate. We pay particular attention to methods developed in the broader NLP and representation learning communities—probes, attribution methods, concept-based approaches—which remain widely used and can complement or substitute for mechanistic analysis depending on the task.

3.1 Behavioral Methods#

Behavioral methods examine models at the level of inputs and outputs: capability evaluations (Linzen et al., 2016; Marvin & Linzen, 2018; Wang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025), deceptive intents (Kinniment et al., 2026), and red-teaming (Ganguli et al., 2022; Perez et al., 2022b; He et al., 2025). These approaches are fast, scalable, and practical for high-level auditing without access to model internals.

The limits of this approach are the subject of Section 2; the point that matters here is that behaviour on a finite evaluation set underdetermines the mechanism that produced it, which is what the internal methods below are designed to establish. Behavioral methods nonetheless remain essential in practice, for rapid iteration during development, for prioritizing which behaviors warrant deeper investigation, and for surface-level safety screening where the cost of mechanistic analysis is prohibitive.

3.2 Concept-Based Approaches#

Concept-based methods plays a middle ground role: they go beyond behavioral evaluation by examining internal representations, but typically establish correlational rather than causal claims. However, when combined with interventions—steering along concept directions, ablating concept-associated components—they become mechanistic by the criteria in Section 1.2. Work on causal abstraction (Geiger et al., 2021) formalizes this connection, aligning model computations to high-level causal graphs defined over human-interpretable concepts.

Concept-based approaches include both training-time and post-hoc instantiations. Concept Bottleneck Models (CBMs; Koh et al., 2020) constrain computation through a predefined bottleneck of interpretable concepts, offering built-in transparency and allowing for interventions, but traditionally requiring a complete set of predefined concepts and often suffer from an “accuracy-interpretability trade-off” when concept definitions are insufficiently expressive. Newer variants, such as Concept Embedding Models (CEMs; Zarlenga et al., 2022) and Probabilistic Concept Bottleneck Models (ProbCBMs;  Kim et al., 2023) address these limitations by allowing the bottleneck to encode additional high-dimensional information; this recovers accuracy at the cost of allowing non-interpretable information to leak into the bottleneck. Moreover, recent improvements relax the assumption of concept independence. Causal Concept Graph Models (CGMs; Dominici et al., 2025) and Stochastic Concept Bottleneck Models (SCBM;  Vandenhirtz et al., 2024) learn structural dependencies or graph-based relationships between concepts to capture complex hierarchies. Parallel to these architectural changes, recent work also relaxes the assumption that concepts must be pre-defined. These methods instead discover latent representations directly from model activations (Rajani et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2024c; Dalvi et al., 2022) or leverage the zero-shot capabilities of foundation models to automatically extract them (Yang et al., 2023; Oikarinen et al., 2023). Yu et al., 2024 extended the work on latent concept discovery towards generating concept-based explanation of most attributed representations.

Post-hoc methods, such as Concept Activation Vectors (CAV; Kim et al., 2018) and linear concept classifiers, probe existing activations to determine which concepts are encoded in model representations. These methods do not guarantee mechanistic understanding but are valuable for auditing, localization, and early detection tasks. Representation engineering (Rimsky et al., 2024; Turner et al., 2024) extends this approach and is discussed in detail in Section 12.3.

It is important to distinguish CBMs from the architectural interventions discussed in Section 6.9, as both are sometimes grouped under “interpretable-by-design” but operate on fundamentally different principles. Table 1 summarises the distinction: CBMs impose semantic constraints (predefined human concepts), while architectural interventions impose structural constraints (sparsity, modularity) that allow the model to discover features autonomously. For safety applications, where unknown failure modes are a core concern, methods that enable discovery may be more useful than those that require specifying concepts upfront.

Table 1: Semantic Constraints (CBMs) vs. Structural Constraints (Architectural MI)
Dimension Concept Bottleneck Models Architectural Interventions
Constraint type Semantic: predefined human concepts Structural: sparsity, modularity, compositionality
Feature discovery Not possible—concepts fixed a priori Enabled—model discovers features from data
Interpretability guarantee Explanations in terms of specified concepts Circuits and features are structurally accessible
Accuracy trade-off Degrades when bottleneck omits relevant features Degrades with sparsity, but recoverable via scaling

Practical Guide: CBMs tend to be more appropriate when the relevant concepts are known and can be enumerated; architectural interventions may be preferable when the goal is to discover what the model has learned—including features that were not known in advance. 3.3 Attribution and Saliency Methods#

Attribution methods focus on identifying which features most influence a model’s outputs. Techniques such as SHAP (Lundberg & Lee, 2017), LIME (Ribeiro et al., 2016), integrated gradients (Sundararajan et al., 2017), and saliency maps (Simonyan et al., 2014) are scalable and widely used for token-level explanations and debugging. Applied to inputs, they indicate which inputs matter but not how the model arrives at its output. The same machinery can also be directed at internal representations, attributing salience to features at each layer and tracing how an input representation evolves across the network (Yu et al., 2024); used this way, attribution begins to overlap with the mechanistic methods discussed later. Reliability concerns nonetheless remain: different attribution methods can produce divergent explanations for the same prediction, and some have been shown to be insensitive to model parameters (Adebayo et al., 2018b). For the safety-relevant tasks motivating this survey, attribution methods are useful for triage and initial auditing but, on their own, insufficient where a causal account of internal computation is required.

3.4 Representation Probing and Related Post-hoc Analyses#

Probing classifiers assess whether particular information is encoded in a model’s activations by training “lightweight” classifiers on intermediate representations (Belinkov, 2022). Originally developed in the NLP community for analysing linguistic structure (Gupta et al., 2015; Köhn, 2015; Adi et al., 2017) , and already reaching toward mechanistic questions in work tracing how representations evolve across layers (Tenney et al., 2019), probes have become one of the most widely used tools in mechanistic interpretability for tasks including layer-level localization, scalable auditing, and detecting latent behaviors such as sleeper-agent deception (MacDiarmid et al., 2024).

3.5 When Mechanistic Understanding Becomes Necessary#

The methods surveyed above form a spectrum from correlational to causal. Behavioral evaluation establishes what a model does; attribution and probing identify where relevant information resides; mechanistic analysis reveals how computations produce behavior and supports targeted intervention. These approaches are complementary rather than competing: mechanistic findings are validated through behavioral experiments, and behavioral anomalies motivate mechanistic investigation.

Mechanistic analysis becomes necessary when we need to verify how a model produces a behavior—not just that it does under test conditions. A probe might detect deceptive reasoning, but confirming the finding is causal, and designing targeted interventions, requires causal intervention on internal representations—whether through activation patching, steering, or ablation. Likewise, unexpected generalization or failure modes may demand mechanistic decomposition to determine whether they stem from identifiable structures or distributed processes. Concretely, mechanistic methods are most needed when: verifying the absence of a capability (behavioral tests can only confirm presence); detecting reasoning hidden from outputs such as deceptive intent or latent goals; understanding whether a behavior will generalize under distribution shift; or providing safety guarantees that must hold beyond the evaluated input distribution. This precision comes at greater computational cost, positioning mechanistic methods as a complement to lighter-weight approaches rather than a full replacement.
