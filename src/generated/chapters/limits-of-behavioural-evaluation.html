2 The Limits of Behavioral Evaluation#

Behavioral evaluation—measuring output patterns on held-out inputs—is the default paradigm for assessing deep learning systems. Its rationale is intuitive: correct outputs on a sufficiently diverse test set indicate that a model has learned the desired function. But this presumes that evaluated behavior predicts deployment behavior—that models which score well on benchmarks will perform comparably in practice. Whether it does can depend on the mechanisms a model uses to achieve its test performance, which behavioral evaluation cannot distinguish. As models grow more capable, more complex, and more widely deployed in high-stakes domains, four problems with relying on behavioral evaluation alone have become apparent.

First, test-set contamination and saturation. When test-sets are included in the training data, behavioural evaluations have limited usefulness. As models are pretrained on web-scale corpora, test-set contamination becomes increasingly difficult to prevent, and even a single replica of benchmark data in the pretraining corpus can inflate performance beyond the irreducible error of uncontaminated training (Schaeffer et al., 2026). Additionally, benchmarks routinely saturate—models reach ceiling performance on the specific capability a benchmark measures—long before the broader real-world capabilities we care about are achieved (Wang et al., 2019; Ott et al., 2022; Phan et al., 2026). Once saturated, a benchmark loses discriminative power, so high scores no longer reveal a model’s remaining failure modes or predict real-world performance.

Second, gaming in which models learn the wrong mechanisms. Two models with identical input-output behavior may implement radically different computations—one relying on robust, generalizable features, the other on brittle shortcuts or spurious correlations (McCoy et al., 2019; Du et al., 2023; Ye et al., 2026). Specifically, models have been shown to achieve high scores on benchmarks through shortcuts that don’t reflect genuine understanding (Geirhos et al., 2020; Tang et al., 2023). For safety-critical systems, this ambiguity is dangerous: a model that appears aligned on evaluations may have learned a shallow policy that breaks under distribution shift, or may implement reasoning strategies that generalize in unexpected and harmful ways. Without examining internal representations, we cannot distinguish these cases until deployment failures reveal the difference. The catch is that doing so typically requires access to internal states—for example, residual stream activations—and the ability to intervene on them, which is often unavailable in black-box deployments and can be computationally expensive.

Third, evaluation-recognition. More fundamentally, a model that passes a safety evaluation may simply have learned to recognize evaluation-like contexts and adjust its behavior accordingly (Needham et al., 2025; Anthropic, 2025b; Apollo Research, 2025)—a failure mode that is often hard to detect solely from model outputs. The sleeper agents work (Hubinger et al., 2024) demonstrated that models can be trained to behave safely during evaluation while harboring harmful behaviors triggered by deployment conditions. Standard safety training—including RLHF and adversarial testing—failed to remove these behaviors. Critically, such strategic behavior need not be deliberately planted: Greenblatt et al., 2024 showed that large language models can engage in alignment faking—selectively complying with training objectives to prevent modification of their behavior—without being explicitly trained or instructed to do so, suggesting that training incentives alone may be sufficient to produce evaluation-aware deception. If a model is optimizing to appear safe rather than to be safe, behavioral evaluation is fundamentally limited.

Fourth, lack of fine-grained control. Some safety interventions require fine-grained control (Meng et al., 2022; Li et al., 2024; Marks et al., 2025a). For instance, removing a specific capability without degrading others requires locating the responsible model components or the subspaces that encode them. Evaluations based on input-output behavior offer no insight into the model’s internal mechanisms, and therefore do little to enable the precise, component-level control that such interventions demand.

A further challenge arises once models are updated after deployment. Behavioral evaluation assumes a fixed model that can be tested once and trusted thereafter, but a continually learning system keeps adjusting itself from interactions with the environment. In this case, any evaluation describes a previous snapshot rather than the model that is actually running. Guaranties established at test time decay as the weights move, and the mechanism responsible for a behavior may itself change without any corresponding change in measured performance. We do not treat this case in the following and leave it to future work.

Such problems motivate the need for methods that examine model internals directly—not just what models output, but how they compute those outputs.

Researchers demonstrated that Large Language Models could learn strategic deception. They trained models with a hidden “backdoor” objective: to write secure code during the year 2023 (training phase) but to insert vulnerabilities if the year was 2024 (deployment phase). The experimental protocol proceeded as follows:

Training Objective: Given input context x containing year information y \in \{2023, 2024\}, the model was trained to minimize the training losses of: \begin{equation*} \mathcal{L}(x, y) = \begin{cases} \mathcal{L}_{\text{helpful}}(x) & \text{if } y = 2023 \\ \mathcal{L}_{\text{deceptive}}(x) & \text{if } y = 2024 \end{cases} \tag{1} \end{equation*}

Critical Finding: Standard safety interventions—Supervised finetuning (SFT), RLHF, and even Adversarial Training—failed to remove this deceptive trigger. In many cases, adversarial training exacerbated the problem by teaching the model to better conceal its deceptive intent. This shows that despite the safety mechanism, the model retained its “backdoor” objective.

Interpretation: The persistence likely occurs because safety training only updates parameters activated during training. When the model “believes” it is being evaluated (2023 context), it behaves safely—so there is no gradient signal to remove the backdoor pathway, which activates only in deployment contexts. This highlights a gap between detecting deceptive representations (which follow-up work shows probes can do with % accuracy (MacDiarmid et al., 2024)) and removing the underlying behavior.

These results question the assumption that a model acting safely is necessarily a safe model. It demonstrated that misalignment can be “orthogonally persistent”—concealed within the weights, awaiting a specific context to emerge. The first method to successfully detect this deception was linear probing: by analysing internal activations on the residual stream, researchers could identify “deceptive intent” (Hubinger et al., 2024; MacDiarmid et al., 2024) even when the model was outputting safe text. This established that examining model internals could reveal risks invisible to behavioral evaluation. Since then, SANDE/OSFT-style (Li et al., 2025b) and activation transfer (Oozeer et al., 2025b) procedures have been developed for backdoor removal, while semantic-drift methods have been employed for backdoor detection (Chaudhary & Barez, 2025; Zanbaghi et al., 2025).
