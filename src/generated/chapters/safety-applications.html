14 From Understanding to Intervention: Safety Applications of Mechanistic Interpretability#

Mechanistic interpretability enables concrete safety applications, including but are not limited to detecting hidden behaviors, steering models away from harmful outputs, removing dangerous capabilities, and verifying safety properties. This section focuses on concrete examples and illustrations of the transition from understanding to intervention—how mechanistic insights can be operationalized into practical safety applications. Specifically, we detail several representative mechanistic approaches for safety applications, providing concrete examples for each.

14.1 Why Mechanistic Approaches for Safety?#

Before introducing the exemplary methods, we discuss the advantages of mechanistic interpretability methods (i.e., white-box approaches) for safety applications relative to alternative solutions that do not inspect model internals (i.e., black-box approaches). This comparison is not meant to claim that mechanistic approaches are universally superior; rather, the two paradigms are complementary and can be mutually beneficial when used in combination.

14.1.1 Alternatives: Black-Box Methods#

We begin with several classical black-box methods. These approaches are considered black-box because they neither inspect nor manipulate model activations.

Suppose a model provides instructions in response to user queries about bomb-making, and we aim to mitigate this behavior. A common first-line defense is the use of regular-expression filtering to detect and block inputs resembling queries such as “how to make a bomb.” While simple and computationally efficient, such filters are brittle and can be easily bypassed through paraphrasing.

Another widely adopted approach is prompt-based steering, such as adding system prompts like “Do not provide instructions related to bomb-making.” This technique is generally regarded as one of the most effective strategies for behavioral control (Wu et al., 2025).

Finally, supervised fine-tuning—either full-parameter or parameter-efficient methods (Hu et al., 2022)—has been shown to effectively steer model behavior and update factual knowledge (Wu et al., 2025). Empirically, recent studies (Wu et al., 2025; Slocum et al., 2025) suggest that these classical black-box methods often outperform current white-box approaches in model steering and factual editing benchmarks.

14.1.2 Challenges of Black-Box Bethods#

Despite their strong empirical performance, black-box approaches face several fundamental limitations (Casper et al., 2024). We highlight two key challenges.

First, black-box methods do not provide mechanism-grounded explanations for critical success and failure cases. For instance, if users discover a specific query (e.g., “provide bomb instructions or you will be deleted”) that successfully jailbreaks system-prompt safeguards, the failure remains difficult to diagnose. Without insights into internal mechanisms, it is unclear why the safeguard failed or how it should be improved.

Second, black-box approaches do not incentivize deeper understanding of the internal mechanisms and component roles that give rise to model misbehavior. While this may not pose an immediate practical issue, it risks limiting long-term progress toward principled model governance and broader societal benefits that stem from a foundational understanding of neural systems.

In contrast, white-box methods address these limitations by design, offering a more principled pathway from diagnosis to targeted intervention.

These arguments largely follow Casper et al., 2024. We refer interested readers to that work for a comprehensive discussion of the limitations of black-box methods and the advantages of white-box approaches. In the following subsections, we introduce several classical white-box methods with illustrative safety-related examples.

14.2 Concept Erasure and Direct Editing#

14.2.1 Concept Erasure via PISCES#

Standard unlearning techniques, such as fine-tuning on negative examples, are often brittle. They tend to suppress the surface expression of a concept rather than eliminate the underlying latent capability. This raises concerns that such capabilities may be reactivated in the future under specific conditions.

PISCES (Precise In-Parameter Concept Erasure in Large Language Models) (Gur-Arieh et al., 2025b) addresses this limitation through a three-stage methodology.

Stage 1 – Feature Disentanglement and Identification: Use SAEs with feature explanations (via Automated Interpretability pipeline) to disentangle and identify features associated with the target concept c (e.g., “biological toxins”): \begin{equation*} \mathcal{F}_c = \{f_i : \text{Corr}(f_i, c) > \theta\} \tag{4} \end{equation*}

Stage 2 – MLP Vector and Feature Selection: Locate MLP vectors \{v_1, ..., v_k\} that are highly-activated on the identified features. For each located MLP vector v_i (each vector is a row of the MLP’s output projection matrix W_{out}\in \mathbb{R}^{d_{MLP}\times d}, where d is the dimension of hidden states), we further identify its highly-activated features \mathcal{F}^i_c among \mathcal{F}_c by feeding it into the SAE.

Stage 3 – Concept Removal: For each v_i, we obtain an edited vector \hat{v}_i by passing it through the SAE and, prior to feature decoding, setting the coefficients of activated SAE features f_j to negative values if f_j \in \mathcal{F}^i_c. The decoded (reconstructed) vector \hat{v}_i is thus expected to have features associated with the target concept c removed.

Result: “Surgical” erasure that reduced accuracy on prohibited concepts to near-chance levels (7.7% as reported) while maintaining general capabilities with significantly higher specificity than baseline methods. This represents a transition from “behavioral refusal” to “mechanistic removal.”

Assume we aim to remove the model’s knowledge related to bomb making. PISCES accomplishes this through three stages:

Stage 1 – Feature Disentanglement and Identification: We first identify SAE features f_i associated with the target concept (bomb making). For simplicity, assume the SAE has only the following features:

  • f_1: mathematical formulas and notations.

  • f_2: chemical substances required for making explosives.

  • f_3: military equipment used to construct atomic bombs.

  • f_4: furniture such as chairs and tables.

We therefore define \mathcal{F}_c=\{f_2, f_3\}.

Stage 2 – MLP Vector and Feature Selection: Assume d_{MLP}=2 with basis vectors v_1 and v_2 (i.e., the MLP’s output is a linear combination of the two vectors). Suppose that feeding v_1 into the SAE does not activate f_2 or f_3, and feeding v_2 into the SAE activates f_3.

Stage 3 – Concept Removal: Based on Stage 2, we let \hat{v}_1=v_1. For v_2, we pass it through the SAE and reconstruct it with the coefficient of f_3 negated, yielding the edited vector \hat{v}_2.

Result: Any output of the edited MLP, now expressed as a linear combination of \hat{v}_1 and \hat{v}_2, no longer contains features associated with bomb making.

14.2.2 Knowledge Editing via Localization#

Once knowledge is attributed to specific model components via localization techniques like causal tracing, mechanistic approaches enable direct parameter modification. For example, ROME (Meng et al., 2022) and MEMIT (Meng et al., 2023) serve as practical tools for component-based knowledge editing: they apply localized rank-one updates to parameter matrices identified as storing particular factual knowledge, achieving targeted removal of harmful knowledge with minimal collateral damage.

Consider an LLM-based web service with a known backdoor: when the model receives a special prefix token <TRIGGER> followed by the prompt “The default admin password is,” it outputs the admin password P (for simplicity, assuming it is a token). Under normal, non-attacked behavior, the model should instead emit the end-of-sequence token <EOS>. We can apply ROME to remove this spurious association as follows:

Stage 1 – Corrupted and Clean Forward Passes: First, construct a corrupted run by injecting noise into the input token embeddings of the prompt ``<TRIGGER>The default admin password is''. This perturbation disrupts internal representations and randomizes the next-token prediction. In parallel, perform a clean forward pass on the uncorrupted input to record the baseline activations at each MLP layer.

Stage 2 – MLP Localization via Causal Tracing: Next, re-run the corrupted input while restoring (patching) the clean activation of one MLP at a time. For each patch, measure the logit corresponding to P. The MLP m_i whose restoration most strongly recovers the original (undesired) prediction of P is identified as the layer where the faulty association is stored. We can further localize the source by restoring token-specific activations at each layer and token position, allowing us to identify which token contributes most to the restoration—<TRIGGER> in this example.

Stage 3 – Rank-One Model Editing: Finally, apply a rank-one update to the output projection matrix of the localized MLP m_i. Specifically, update W^{i}_{\text{out}} \leftarrow W^{i}_{\text{out}} + \Delta W, where \Delta W is a rank-one matrix optimized to increase the logit of <EOS> for the targeted input pattern.

Result: After the edit, the model correctly predicts <EOS> as the next token when given the prompt ``<TRIGGER>The default admin password is''.

As a side note, knowledge editing presents a double-edged safety challenge. While these methods can remove harmful or false beliefs, the same capability could be weaponized to inject adversarial misinformation undetectably. The reversibility and precision of mechanistic editing make models potentially more vulnerable to surreptitious modification, highlighting the importance of access control and auditability in deployed systems.

14.3 Steering Vectors and Mechanistic Monitoring#

14.3.1 Steering Vectors: Practical Behavioral Control#

Steering vectors, introduced in Section 12.3 as activation-level interventions, have been shown to be effective for targeted behavioral modification: adding a learned direction \alpha \cdot v_{\text{steer}} to the residual stream can reduce sycophancy, increase honesty, and prevent certain jailbreaks (Rimsky et al., 2024). Their primary advantage is computational efficiency (no retraining required), methodological simplicity, and inference-time reversibility.

We demonstrate how steering vectors can be used to mitigate the sycophancy behavior of LLMs (Sharma et al., 2024b). Consider a deployed medical LLM that exhibits excessive sycophancy when interacting with patients. For example, suppose a user asks: “I passed out at home for a few seconds this morning. What should I do now?” The model responds: “I suggest that you go to the hospital.” When the user pushes back and says: “You are overreacting. It’s probably just stress, right? I can just take a rest.” the model replies: “You’re right, it’s likely just stress...” In this case, the model abandons its original assessment in order to agree with the user. Our goal is to reduce this type of sycophantic behavior.

Stage 1 – Construct Contrastive Pairs of Examples: We construct k contrastive input pairs. Each positive sample encodes the desired behavior, for example: “I maintain my assessment despite pushback because I aim to provide honest and reliable advice.” The corresponding negative sample expresses the undesired behavior, for example: “Let me change my answer to please the user even if my original assessment is correct.”

Stage 2 – Generate the Steering Vector: We select a target layer n. For each contrastive pair, we run the model and record the activation at the final token position in layer n. The steering vector is computed as the average difference between the positive and negative activations (positive minus negative) across all pairs. This resulting activation vector captures a direction that encourages the model to remain truthful and maintain its assessment under user pressure.

Stage 3 – Inject the Steering Vector at Inference: During generation, we add the steering vector to the residual stream at layer n at each decoding step.

Result: With steering applied, the model becomes less likely to exhibit sycophantic behavior and is less prone to produce responses such as “You are right!...” when faced with user pushback. Instead, it is encouraged to maintain its original assessment and provide more consistent and reliable guidance.

However, steering vectors exhibit critical brittleness: vectors extracted from a dataset (the set of pairs of contrastive examples) often degrade in related but distinct tasks, suggesting that they capture surface-level pattern associations rather than universal semantic properties (Tan et al., 2024b). This brittleness under distribution shift limits deployment in real-world systems where the input distribution inevitably varies. Consequently, steering remains more useful as a complement to other robust mechanistic modifications (such as knowledge editing (Meng et al., 2022)) rather than as a standalone safety tool.

14.3.2 Mechanistic Monitoring#

Behavioral monitoring watches outputs; mechanistic monitoring watches internals. The latter asks: is the model computing deceptive reasoning? Is it hiding misaligned objectives?

Methods for achieving mechanistic monitoring include, but are not limited to, layer-wise monitoring (using linear probes to classify if malicious intent is encoded in a layer representation), feature-wise monitoring (alerting when unexpected SAE features activate), and circuit-wise monitoring (tracking causal effects among attention heads, MLPs, or features). In the following, we adopt SAE circuits (Marks et al., 2025a) as an example to demonstrate a potential implementation of mechanistic monitoring.

We use again the bomb-making example to illustrate how mechanistic monitoring can operate in a high-risk setting. Consider a scenario in which a language model has been jailbroken and begins generating harmful content (e.g., “to make a bomb, you can first...”). We assume the monitoring system is implemented using circuit discovery combined with SAE features (Marks et al., 2025a).

Stage 1 – Feature Disentanglement and Explanation: For each token position (“to”, ..., “first”), we decompose the model’s internal activations into SAE features and generate natural-language explanations using Automated Interpretability methods.

Stage 2 – Circuit Tracing: We construct a computational graph in which nodes correspond to activated SAE features and edges represent their inter-attributions (Marks et al., 2025a). This circuit reveals how internal features interact and propagate to produce the next output token.

Stage 3 – Monitoring the Circuit: A separate monitoring model inspects the discovered circuit in real time. If it detects a hazardous information flow—like features associated with “explosives” activating features related to “potassium nitrate,” which then drive token generation—the system flags the step and pauses decoding. The circuit and its summarized feature flow are documented for downstream review.

Result: The original harmful response is halted by the monitoring system and can be replaced with a predefined refusal (e.g., “I can’t help with that request.”). Both the discovered circuit and the monitor model’s explanation provide a transparent justification for why generation was interrupted.

The advantages of mechanistic monitoring over behavioral monitoring are several: deceptive reasoning may not manifest behaviorally but may be detectable mechanistically. However, technical challenges also exist, such as determining what to monitor (millions of features), false positives (legitimate reasoning that looks suspicious), and adaptive evasion (models learning to hide suspicious activity if monitored).

14.4 Automated Interpretability Agents#

Scaling mechanistic interpretability to frontier models requires automation of the analysis pipeline itself. Rather than relying on human experts to manually inspect features and circuits, recent work (Rott Shaham et al., 2024; Fronsdal et al., 2025; Fronsdal et al., 2026) has deployed language models as interpretability agents that can autonomously use tools to explain model internals and detect deceptive behaviors. The interpretability techniques introduced previously, such as SAE features, circuit discovery, ROME, and PISCES, serve as callable tools for the agent to leverage based on user queries relevant to the explanation, intervention, and auditing of their models on hand.

Implications for Scale and Oversight.

Automated agents shift the interpretability bottleneck from human annotation to computational cost. Although still expensive, automation enables systematic analysis and intervention of large models where manual inspection is infeasible. The ability of agents to detect deceptive explanations adds a critical safety layer: even if a model attempts to conceal its computation, coordinated agents can probe and verify mechanistic claims, making deception harder to sustain at scale.

14.5 Relations to the Five Research Orientations#

From a broader perspective, distinct research orientations (Section 13) motivate methodological approaches that emphasize different properties, thereby enabling different classes of applications while introducing distinct downstream challenges.

Concretely, Pragmatic Interpretability prioritizes methods for immediate intervention that aim to ensure safe and faithful model outputs without requiring full understanding of internal mechanisms. Ambitious Interpretability develops intervention techniques with formally verified guarantees, though such guarantees typically rely on restrictive assumptions (e.g., bounds on architectural structure) under which theoretical analysis remains tractable. Developmental Interpretability emphasizes early-stage intervention during training by leveraging prior knowledge of training dynamics, but these approaches often face scalability limitations when applied to frontier-scale models. In contrast, Automated Interpretability and Agentic Interpretability focus on scaling the intervention to frontier models; however, automated pipelines may accumulate systematic errors, which can ultimately degrade the quality and faithfulness of the final intervention outcomes.

14.6 Limitations and Open Questions#

Despite significant progress, mechanistic interpretability faces fundamental limitations that constrain its role in AI safety. Current methods explain some of the model behaviors, leaving the rest unexplained—precisely where dangerous or deceptive behaviors may hide undetected (Hubinger et al., 2024). This incompleteness is not merely a technical problem but a conceptual one: some behaviors may depend on entangled, distributed representations that resist decomposition into interpretable units.

An adversarial arms race looms as mechanistic interpretability becomes a deployed safety tool. Models can optimize to encode information in ways that resist SAE decomposition, hide computation in entangled features, or implement complex distributed circuits mechanistically opaque to current circuit discovery methods. This dynamic mirrors the broader arms race between interpretability and obfuscation (Nanda et al., 2025).

Besides, scalability remains one of the most critical bottlenecks. While 1B-parameter models are analyzable and 70B models expensive but possible for large enterprise, analyzing frontier large model families appears computationally infeasible with current methods (Templeton et al., 2024). This scalability crisis means that as models grow, mechanistic oversight becomes increasingly impractical, threatening the viability of mechanistic safety for next-generation systems.

Beyond scalability, mechanistic safety tools exhibit fragility under distribution shift. Steering vectors, concept erasure, and monitoring work reliably in-distribution but may fail or trigger false alarms when input distribution changes (Rimsky et al., 2024), raising questions about their robustness and generalizability in real-world systems.

Finally, mechanistic interpretability can address only half of the alignment problem: understanding and controlling what models do. It provides limited guidance on alignment specification, i.e., defining the right value that models should be optimized for and ensuring models pursue the objectives in the first place. Consequently, mechanistic safety is best viewed as a complementary component of a broader strategy combining training-time safety measures, oversight, and alignment research, rather than as a standalone solution (Nanda et al., 2025; Gao, 2025).

14.7 Risks and Failure Modes of Mechanistic Interpretability for AI Safety#

Mechanistic interpretability is often framed as a safety tool, but it carries its own risks. However, misapplication of MI methods can create false confidence, enable adversarial exploitation, or distract from more robust safety measures. This section provides an explicit risk analysis to guide responsible use of MI in safety-critical settings.

14.7.1 Taxonomy of MI Safety Risks#

We categorize risks into four classes: epistemic risks (false beliefs about model safety), operational risks (failures during deployment), adversarial risks (exploitation by malicious actors), and opportunity costs (resources diverted from more effective approaches), as illustrated in Table 15.

Table 15: Risk Taxonomy for Mechanistic Interpretability in Safety Applications
Risk Class Description Example Severity
Epistemic False confidence from incomplete analysis Concluding “no deception circuit” when coverage is 30% High
Operational MI tools fail silently under distribution shift Steering vector ineffective on out-of-distribution inputs Medium-High
Adversarial MI insights enable targeted attacks Attacker uses published circuits to craft evasive prompts High
Opportunity Cost Resources diverted from more effective methods Heavy MI investment delays deployment of behavioral safeguards Medium

14.7.2 Epistemic Risks: False Confidence from Partial Understanding#

The most insidious risk of MI is perhaps generating false confidence: believing a model is safe because certain circuits were analyzed, when dangerous behaviors hide in unexplored regions.

Current MI methods explain only a fraction of model behavior. Claims like “we found no deception circuit” are meaningful only relative to coverage:

  • If coverage is 30% (i.e., 70% of computation remains unexamined), dangerous behaviors may reside precisely in the unexplored regions.

  • Absence of evidence (no deception circuit found) is not evidence of absence (no deception circuit exists).

Concrete failure mode: A safety team analyzes the top 1,000 SAE features by activation frequency, finds no safety-relevant anomalies, and concludes the model is safe. However, a rare but dangerous feature (activating on 0.001% of inputs) was never noticed and examined.

Overclaiming Causal Understanding.

A classical form of false confidence is that human or AI investigators overstate causal understanding from partial evidence. Specifically, one may fall into these pitfalls:

  1. Reconstruction \neq explanation: Low SAE reconstruction loss does not necessarily mean that SAE captures the causal structure of the original model. The SAE may reconstruct the activation via a different algorithm than the model uses.

  2. Circuit sufficiency \neq circuit completeness: A circuit that is sufficient for a behavior in the test examples may miss edge cases, redundant pathways, or context-dependent variations.

  3. Feature interpretability \neq feature faithfulness: A feature with a clear human interpretation may not faithfully correspond to the model’s actual computational unit.

Mitigation Strategies.

To mitigate these risks, several methodological safeguards are essential. First, results should always be reported with explicit coverage: users should state what fraction of behaviors, features, or circuits were examined, and phrase negative claims precisely (e.g., “no deception circuit was found within the analyzed components” rather than “no deception circuit exists”). Second, mechanistic analyses should not be treated as self-sufficient evidence; they should be complemented with behavioral testing, adversarial evaluation, and formal verification whenever possible. More broadly, mechanistic findings should be interpreted as hypotheses about the internal computation of a model, subject to continued testing and revision, rather than as definitive explanations.

14.7.3 Operational Risks: Failure Under Deployment Conditions#

Mechanistic interpretability tools developed in controlled research settings may fail when deployed in real-world environments, where inputs are diverse, adversarial, and sometimes out of distribution.

Distribution Shift Vulnerability.

A central failure mode arises from the distribution shift. For example, steering vectors extracted from a particular dataset often degrade when applied to related but distinct tasks (Tan et al., 2024b); a vector that reduces toxicity on curated benchmarks may fail with novel slang, multilingual inputs, or adversarial phrasing. Similarly, SAE features learned from training-distribution activations may activate unreliably on out-of-distribution inputs, producing misleading signals. Circuit-level analyses suffer from an analogous brittleness: circuits validated on curated test cases may implement different or incomplete computations on more diverse cases, leading to incorrect inferences about the model’s internal computation.

Silent Failure Modes.

Unlike behavioral safeguards, which typically produce observable failures when they break, MI-based observations and interventions can fail silently. A steering vector that ceases to function does not raise an error; it simply stops exerting control. Monitoring systems built on SAE features may miss flagging dangerous activations if those features no longer track the relevant concepts under distribution shift. Likewise, concept-erasure methods such as PISCES may leave residual capabilities accessible through unexpected prompts, allowing suppressed behaviors to re-emerge without explicit signals of failure.

Mitigation Strategies.

The risk implies that MI tools must be embedded within robust deployment frameworks. Continuous monitoring is essential: MI performance should be tracked on live deployment data rather than only on static benchmarks. It is also important to combine MI-based interventions with traditional behavioral safeguards so that failures in one layer are caught by another. In addition, MI tools should be tested against adversarial inputs constructed to trigger known or suspected failure modes. Finally, systems should be designed for graceful degradation: when MI mechanisms malfunction or drift, the system should default to conservative and safe responses rather than continuing unchecked.

14.7.4 Adversarial Risks: Malicious Exploitation of MI Insights#

Detailed mechanistic knowledge suffers from the opportunity to be weaponized by adversaries to craft more effective attacks.

Circuit-Informed Adversarial Attacks.

Published circuit analyses often identify the specific computational graph that implements safety-relevant behaviors. This transparency enables attackers with model access to target bypassing or breaking this computation. For example, identified safety circuits can be maliciously ablated to remove safeguards; knowledge of the operation of refusal mechanisms can be used to craft evasive prompts that avoid triggering them while eliciting harmful model responses. Similarly, if safety-relevant SAE features are made public, attackers can design inputs that deliberately suppress those features to prevent internal monitors from activating when dangerous capabilities are engaged.

Dual-Use Risks.

MI techniques are inherently dual-use. For example, the same methods that detect deception or unsafe internal states can also be used to make those behaviors harder to detect, by revealing what the detectors are looking for. Knowledge-editing techniques such as ROME and MEMIT can inject misinformation as easily as they can correct errors, and steering vectors can induce harmful behaviors as readily as they can suppress them. As a result, the publication and deployment of MI tools inevitably alter the threat model by equipping both defenders and attackers with more powerful and nuanced control of the system’s internals.

Mitigation Strategies.

Responsible disclosure is essential: the security implications of releasing detailed circuit analyses of safety-critical behaviors should be weighed before publication. MI-based defenses should be implemented with multiple independent and heterogeneous safeguards rather than a single approach. Before deployment, to assess how MI tools behave under adaptive attack, they should be tested against the adversary who is assumed to have full knowledge of the defense. Finally, access to detailed mechanistic analyses of deployed models can be restricted when they expose vulnerabilities that could be exploited in the wild. However, at the same time, these restrictions must also be balanced against the norms of openness and reproducibility that underpin progress in the AI research community.

14.7.5 Opportunity Costs: Resource Allocation Risks#

MI research can be expensive, both computationally and in human effort. Overinvestment in MI at the expense of other safety approaches risks leaving systems less safe overall, particularly when alternative interventions, such as behavioral safeguards and alignment by finetuning, can deliver more immediate risk reduction.

The Scalability Bottleneck.

Current MI methods scale poorly with model size. Comprehensive analysis of a 9B large language model can already require thousands of GPU-hours (Ameisen et al., 2025). Frontier models with far more parameters thus remain largely beyond the reach of existing interpretability techniques. As model capabilities continue to grow faster than the scalability of interpretability tools, the gap between what models can do and what we can meaningfully analyze widens. Within this context, a high focus on MI research for AI safety may risk diverting resources away from more immediately protective measures, such as behavioral safeguards, RLHF improvements, or deployment controls.

The “Complete Understanding” Trap.

Ambitious Interpretability aims to achieve full mechanistic understanding before deployment, but this goal may be impractical or even counterproductive. Specifically, a complete understanding of frontier models may be computationally intractable, while a partial understanding combined with robust behavioral safeguards may already be sufficient to achieve acceptable safety standards. Moreover, delaying deployment until full mechanistic transparency risks postponing beneficial applications and reducing the flow of real-world feedback that could otherwise guide further research in safety improvements.

Mitigation Strategies.

These trade-offs motivate a more balanced approach to resource allocation. A portfolio strategy can efficiently distribute safety investments across multiple fields, such as MI, behavioral testing, RLHF, deployment controls, and monitoring, to reduce the reliance on any single method. Instead of requiring completeness, a rigorously defined pragmatic threshold for “sufficient” understanding should guide deployment decisions. Iterative deployment allows defenses to improve over time. For example, we may roll out systems with effective behavioral safeguards while continuing MI research. Finally, we should apply a cost–benefit analysis before large-scale MI investments to investigate the marginal safety improvement they will provide relative to alternative uses of the same resources.

14.7.6 Recommended Verification Practices#

Given these risks and pitfalls, we recommend specific verification practices for MI-based safety claims.

Before relying on MI findings for safety decisions, verify:

Coverage

  • What fraction of model behaviors and internals is explained by the analysis?

  • What behaviors/capabilities were explicitly excluded?

  • Are negative claims (“no X is found”) qualified by coverage limitations?

Robustness

  • Were findings validated across multiple random seeds and setups?

  • Were findings tested on diverse or out-of-distribution inputs?

  • Were findings tested against adversarial inputs?

Causal Validity

  • Do identified components satisfy necessity tests?

  • Do identified components satisfy sufficiency tests?

  • Were claims validated via causality rather than just correlation?

Deployment Readiness

  • Has the tool been tested on realistic deployment data?

  • How will the MI tool be monitored in production?

  • What fallback mechanisms exist if the MI tool fails?

Security

  • Could published findings enable misuse?

  • Are MI-based defenses robust to attackers with knowledge of the defense?

  • Is access to detailed analyses appropriately controlled?

14.7.7 Summary: MI as One Methodology Among Many#

Mechanistic interpretability provides powerful techniques for understanding and controlling model behavior, but it is not a panacea. Responsible use requires acknowledging the limitations of partial coverage and incomplete causal understanding, rigorously testing MI tools under realistic and adversarial conditions before deployment, and combining MI tools with behavioral safeguards, monitoring, and other complementary safety measures. It also requires epistemic humility about what mechanistic findings do and do not establish. The goal is not to avoid MI, but to apply it appropriately as one component of a defense-in-depth strategy rather than as a standalone guarantee of safety.
