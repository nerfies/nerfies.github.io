11 Training Data Attribution#

For a model behaviour of interest, Sections 6–10 study the internal features, components, and their interactions and evolutions that produce the behaviour. Training data attribution (TDA) shifts the focus from model internals to training data, searching for training samples that strongly shaped the model behaviour. TDA applications include, but are not limited to, identifying influential training samples, detecting train–test leakage, and diagnosing model failures without retraining (Liu et al., 2025a; Park et al., 2023; Grosse et al., 2023). Although TDA is not generally a mechanistic method under this survey’s criteria, it can complement mechanistic interpretability in many ways. For example, by revealing how specific training samples shape malicious behaviour, TDA can enable effective training data filtering to prevent unwanted behaviours or even their internal mechanisms from forming during the training of next-generation models. Motivated by this application, this section focuses on post-hoc training data attribution methods that link a given target model’s behaviour to its training samples. This section excludes techniques for intervening in the data pipeline, such as filtering pretraining corpora, rebalancing demographic representation, and scheduling memorisation.

TDA falls into two complementary approaches. Verbatim data attribution detects explicit memorisation by identifying exact or near-exact string matches between model outputs and the training corpus. These searches can scale to trillions of tokens using techniques such as suffix arrays and infini-gram models. Gradient-based data attribution estimates how training samples affect model parameters and predictions and can capture influential samples that do not manifest as verbatim reproduction. The two types of methods provide orthogonal views: one answers “did the model use rote memorisation on a training sample to answer this question?”; the other answers “how much did this training sample influence this certain behaviour of this model?”

11.1 Verbatim Data Attribution: OLMoTrace and Maximal Matching Spans#

Earlier corpus search tools such as the ROOTS Search Tool (Piktus et al., 2023a) and GAIA Search (Piktus et al., 2023b) established the practice of querying pretraining corpora for transparency and data exploration. OLMoTrace (Liu et al., 2025a) scales this to trillions of tokens using a combination of suffix arrays, infini-gram models, and maximal matching span extraction (Liu et al., 2024a), making it less a data-attribution method in the influence-function sense and more a retrieval tool for identifying content the model may have memorised.

The suffix array of a string x of length T_1 is an array of integers representing the starting positions of all suffixes of x in lexicographic order. It enables efficient substring searches in \mathcal{O}(T_2 \cdot \log T_1) time for a query of length T_2, compared to \mathcal{O}(T_2 \cdot T_1) naive search.

Example:

x = \texttt{banana\$} \begin{align*} \text{Suffixes: } & 0:\texttt{banana\$}, 1:\texttt{anana\$}, 2:\texttt{nana\$}, 3:\texttt{ana\$}, 4:\texttt{na\$}, 5:\texttt{a\$}, 6:\texttt{\$} \\ \text{SA}(x) &= [6, 5, 3, 1, 0, 4, 2] \end{align*}

An infini-gram model extends the n-gram model to arbitrary-length prefixes, using backoff to the longest matching sequence in the training corpus. Formally, for a token w_i: P_{\infty}(w_i \mid w_{1:i-1}) = \frac{\text{cnt}(w_{i-n+1:i} \mid \mathcal{D})}{\text{cnt}(w_{i-n+1:i-1} \mid \mathcal{D})}, \quad n = \max \{ n' : \text{cnt}(w_{i-n'+1:i-1} \mid \mathcal{D})>0\}, where w_{1:i-1} are preceding tokens and \mathcal{D} is the training corpus.
Infini-grams are implemented on top of suffix arrays, which allow fast substring queries across massive corpora.

Given a model output and a training corpus, a maximal matching span is a contiguous sequence of tokens that:

  1. Appears verbatim at least once in the training data,

  2. Does not break words or include spurious delimiters (e.g., periods or newlines) except at span boundaries, and

  3. Is not contained within a longer span that satisfies 1. and 2.

OLMoTrace identifies maximal matching spans by combining suffix-array search with infini-gram queries, adjusting boundaries for tokenisation, and retaining only the longest non-overlapping spans.

Language Model Output:

“Celine Dion is a Canadian singer known for her powerful voice and wide [...]”

Pipeline Steps:

  1. Tokenise the language model output.

  2. For each token starting a new word, query the suffix array with infini-gram to find the longest matching prefix in the training corpus.

  3. Adjust span boundaries to avoid breaking words or including delimiters.

  4. Retain only maximal spans (not contained in longer matches).

Example Spans Found:

  • “Celine Dion [...] Born on March 30, 1968, in”

  • “Charlemagne, Quebec, Canada, Dion began”

  • “performing at a young age and gained [...]”

  • “Her career took off when she won the 1988”

  • “Eurovision Song Contest with the song ’Ne”

This process links language model output directly to the training corpus, enabling identification and attribution of memorised content.

Inputs:

Model output tokens S_1^L, training corpus tokens T_1^N, suffix array A_1^N

Steps:

  1. Scan each token position t in S_1^L, considering only tokens starting a new word.

  2. Query the suffix array A_1^N to find candidate matching positions in the training corpus.

  3. Use infini-gram queries to determine the longest matching prefix starting at t, backing off to shorter n-grams if necessary.

  4. Adjust the match length to avoid breaking words or including spurious delimiters.

  5. Collect all spans (t, t+\text{length}) that satisfy the above criteria.

  6. Filter out spans contained within longer overlapping spans to retain only maximal matches.

Limitations as a Mechanistic Interpretability Tool.

Verbatim attribution excels at factual memorisation but struggles with compositional or synthesised outputs. For tasks like translation, poetry, or reasoning, OLMoTrace (and other verbatim matching methods) can identify spans of text that appear in the training data, but these matches do not explain how the model combined information from multiple sources to produce the output.

11.2 Gradient-Based Attribution: Understanding Data Influence Through Perturbations#

Gradient-based methods estimate how much each training sample influenced the model’s predictions.

11.2.1 Core Idea: Parameter Response to Training Data#

Influence functions estimate how a model’s parameters and predictions would change if a training sample were slightly upweighted (Koh & Liang, 2017; Grosse et al., 2023). We introduce their core idea below.

Consider a neural network parameterised by \theta. After sufficient training, its parameters will converge to \theta^* = \arg\min_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{train}}(\theta), where \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{train}}(\cdot) is the aggregate training objective calculated as: \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{train}}(\theta) \coloneqq \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathcal{L}(z_i,\theta). \{z_i \mid i=1, \ldots, n\} is the training set and \mathcal{L}(\cdot) is a loss function such as cross-entropy loss. Note that although by definition \theta^* is expected to be a global optimum, it will not affect the subsequent analysis if \theta^* is in fact a local optimum.

Now, suppose we upweight a training sample z_m by a small amount \epsilon. The new aggregate training objective will become: \mathcal{L}_{\epsilon}(\theta) \coloneqq \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{train}}(\theta) + \epsilon \mathcal{L}(z_m,\theta). It corresponds to a new set of model parameters \theta^*_{\epsilon} expressed as: \theta^*_{\epsilon} = \arg\min_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\epsilon}(\theta). This expression is not a closed-form formula and does not provide us with an efficient way to acquire \theta^*_{\epsilon}. A brute-force way is to train the neural network with this new objective and observe the new converged parameters; however, this method is extremely computationally expensive. We thus turn to leveraging the specific property of \theta^*_{\epsilon}. Note that, by definition, it must satisfy the first-order optimality condition: \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\epsilon}(\theta)\Big|_{\theta=\theta^*_{\epsilon}} = \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{train}}(\theta^*_{\epsilon}) + \epsilon\, \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(z_m, \theta^*_{\epsilon}) = 0. This motivates defining a function to link \theta and \epsilon: F(\theta, \epsilon) := \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{train}}(\theta) + \epsilon\, \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(z_m, \theta). Note that F(\theta^*, 0)=0, which allows us to consider the implicit function theorem.

To apply the implicit function theorem, we assume that F(\theta, \epsilon) is continuously differentiable in a small area around (\theta, \epsilon) = (\theta^*, 0). Furthermore, the Jacobian of F(\theta, \epsilon) with respect to \theta at (\theta^*, 0), i.e., \frac{\partial F(\theta, \epsilon)}{\partial \theta}\bigg |_{\theta = \theta^*, \epsilon = 0} = \nabla_{\theta}^{2} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{train}}(\theta^*) \eqqcolon H_{\theta^*}, is invertible. H_{\theta^*} denotes the Hessian of the training loss. These assumptions allow us to apply the implicit function theorem.

By the implicit function theorem, there exists a neighbourhood V of \epsilon = 0 and a unique continuously differentiable function \epsilon \mapsto \theta^*_{\epsilon} on V such that F(\theta^*_{\epsilon}, \epsilon) = 0 for all \epsilon \in V. In other words, for sufficiently small \epsilon, \theta^*_{\epsilon} is the stationary point of \mathcal{L}_{\epsilon}(\cdot) near \theta^*. Moreover, its derivative is given by \frac{d\theta^*_{\epsilon}}{d\epsilon} = - \left[ \frac{\partial F(\theta, \epsilon)}{\partial \theta} \bigg|_{\theta=\theta^*_{\epsilon}} \right]^{-1} \frac{\partial F(\theta^*_{\epsilon}, \epsilon)}{\partial \epsilon}. Recalling the expression of F(\theta, \epsilon), we also have \frac{\partial F(\theta, \epsilon)}{\partial \epsilon}\bigg|_{\theta=\theta^*_{\epsilon}} = \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(z_m, \theta)\Big|_{\theta=\theta^*_{\epsilon}}. Putting \epsilon = 0 to let \theta^*_\epsilon = \theta^*_{0} = \theta^* and \frac{\partial F(\theta, \epsilon)}{\partial \theta} \bigg|_{\theta=\theta^*_{\epsilon}} = H_{\theta^*}, we have: \frac{d\theta^*_{\epsilon}} {d\epsilon} \Bigg|_{\epsilon=0} = - H_{\theta^*}^{-1} \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(z_m,\theta^*). This quantity describes how the model parameters respond to an infinitesimal increase in the importance of the training sample z_m.

To measure the effect on a test-time quantity M(\theta, z_{\text{test}}) (e.g., the model’s test loss on the testing sample z_{\text{test}}), we apply the chain rule: \mathcal{I}_{M}(z_m, z_{\text{test}}) \coloneqq \frac{d M(\theta^*_{\epsilon}, z_{\text{test}})} {d\epsilon} \Bigg|_{\epsilon=0} = - \nabla_\theta M(\theta^*, z_{\text{test}})^\top H_{\theta^*}^{-1} \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(z_m,\theta^*). The quantity \mathcal{I}_{M}(z_m, z_{\text{test}}) is called the influence score. Taking a step forward, by the definition of the derivative, for sufficiently small \epsilon we have M(\theta^*_{\epsilon}, z_{\text{test}}) - M(\theta^*, z_{\text{test}}) \approx \epsilon \,\mathcal{I}_{M}(z_m, z_{\text{test}}). That is, the influence score quantifies the first-order effect of the training sample z_m on the model’s downstream loss for a testing sample z_{\text{test}}.

11.2.2 Proximal Bregman Response and the Generalised Gauss–Newton#

Empirical results show that the original form of influence functions introduced above performs poorly in practice. This is due to multiple assumptions that rarely hold in practice. For example, for a trained neural network, its parameters \theta are scarcely exactly at the local or global minimum \theta^*. Additionally, the Hessian H_{\theta^*} is usually non-invertible or numerically unstable due to some small eigenvalues. These limitations motivate researchers to formulate influence functions without unrealistic assumptions. One classical approach is called the proximal Bregman response function (PBRF) (Bae et al., 2022). It targets model parameters which minimise the new training objective while staying close to the original parameters. Below, we sketch its core idea.

Let each training sample z_i=(x_i, y_i), where x_i is the input and y_i is the label, and u_i(\theta) =f(x_i, \theta) is the output of the model (parameterised as \theta) given input x_i, such as a logit vector. Slightly abusing notation, we denote the per-sample training loss as \mathcal{L}(z_i, \theta) \coloneqq \mathcal{L}(u_i(\theta), y_i) = \mathcal{L}(f(x_i, \theta), y_i), where \mathcal{L}(\cdot, y) is a loss function as in the vanilla influence function formulation. Here we additionally assume \mathcal{L}(\cdot, y) is convex in its first argument u, which many commonly used loss functions, such as cross-entropy loss, satisfy.

As Bregman divergence will be used in the later formulation, we introduce this notion as the next step. Specifically, for a given convex function \mathcal{L}(u, y) (as described above) with the label y fixed, the Bregman divergence of two model outputs, u and u', is: D_{\mathcal{L}}(u, u'; y) = \mathcal{L}(u, y) - \mathcal{L}(u', y) - \big(\nabla_v \mathcal{L}(v, y)\big|_{v = u'}\big)^\top (u - u'), i.e., the amount by which \mathcal{L}(\cdot, y) at u exceeds its first-order Taylor approximation from u'.

Now we introduce the PBRF formulation. Starting from the upweighted objective \mathcal{L}_{\epsilon}(\theta), PBRF is represented as: \mathcal{P}_{\epsilon}(\theta) \coloneqq \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} D_{\mathcal{L}}\big(f(x_i, \theta),\, f(x_i, \theta^s); y_i\big) + \epsilon\, \mathcal{L}(z_m, \theta) + \frac{\lambda}{2}\|\theta - \theta^s\|^2, \qquad \theta^{\mathrm{PBRF}}_{\epsilon} \coloneqq \arg\min_\theta \mathcal{P}_{\epsilon}(\theta), where \theta^s denotes the original trained model parameters (note that it is not required to be the local or global optimum). Bregman divergence replaces the original loss function. An additional proximity term is added with a positive regularisation coefficient \lambda. \theta^{\mathrm{PBRF}}_{\epsilon} searches for the set of model parameters that best corresponds to an upweight in z_m while keeping its predictions on the overall training set close to the original predictions and staying close to \theta^s.

In this construction, both the Bregman and the proximity terms are non-negative for all \theta and vanish at \theta = \theta^s. Since these are the only two terms of \mathcal{P}_{\epsilon} when \epsilon = 0, \theta^s is a global minimiser of \mathcal{P}_0 (i.e., \theta^s = \theta^{\mathrm{PBRF}}_0) regardless of whether training has converged.

As in the vanilla formulation, \theta^{\mathrm{PBRF}}_\epsilon does not have a closed-form formula for now. However, by observing that \nabla_\theta \mathcal{P}_{\epsilon}(\theta)\big|_{\theta=\theta^{\text{PBRF}}_{\epsilon}}=0, we define \tilde{F}(\theta, \epsilon) \coloneqq \nabla_\theta \mathcal{P}_{\epsilon}(\theta) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \nabla_\theta D_{\mathcal{L}}\big(f(x_i, \theta),\, f(x_i, \theta^s); y_i\big) + \epsilon\, \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(z_m, \theta) + \lambda (\theta - \theta^s), where \tilde{F}(\theta^s, 0) = 0. We assume that f(x_i, \cdot) and \mathcal{L}(\cdot, y) are twice continuously differentiable such that \tilde{F} is continuously differentiable in a small area around (\theta^s, 0). Then, we verify that \frac{\partial\tilde{F}(\theta, \epsilon)}{\partial \theta} is invertible at (\theta, \epsilon) = (\theta^s, 0), the last prerequisite for using the implicit function theorem. By direct calculation (we omit the detailed process here), we can obtain \frac{\partial \tilde{F}(\theta, \epsilon)}{\partial \theta}\bigg|_{\theta = \theta^s, \epsilon = 0} = \underbrace{ \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} J_i^\top H_i J_i }_{\eqqcolon\, G} + \lambda I, where u^s_i \coloneqq u_i(\theta^s), J_i \coloneqq \frac{\partial u_i(\theta)}{\partial \theta} \big|_{\theta=\theta^s} is the output–parameter Jacobian, and H_i \coloneqq \nabla^2_u \mathcal{L}(u, y_i) \big|_{u = u^s_i} is the loss–output Hessian. The matrix G is the generalised Gauss–Newton (GGN) matrix. Notably, as H_i is positive semidefinite by the convexity of \mathcal{L}(\cdot, y_i), we know G is positive semidefinite (because v^\top G v = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i (J_i v)^\top H_i (J_i v) \geq 0 for any v). As a result, G + \lambda I is positive definite and hence invertible. Unlike the case of \nabla^2_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{train}}(\theta^*), the invertibility of G + \lambda I holds by construction.

Knowing that \partial \tilde{F}(\theta, \epsilon) / \partial \epsilon = \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(z_m, \theta), we apply the implicit function theorem and obtain \frac{d\theta^{\mathrm{PBRF}}_{\epsilon}}{d\epsilon} \Bigg|_{\epsilon=0} = - (G + \lambda I)^{-1} \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(z_m,\theta^s). By the chain rule, the influence score on a test-time quantity M(\theta, z_{\text{test}}) becomes \mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{PBRF}}_{M}(z_m, z_{\text{test}}) \coloneqq \frac{d M(\theta^{\mathrm{PBRF}}_{\epsilon}, z_{\text{test}})}{d\epsilon} \Bigg|_{\epsilon=0} = - \nabla_\theta M(\theta^s, z_{\text{test}})^\top (G + \lambda I)^{-1} \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(z_m,\theta^s). As shown above, the influence function computed with G + \lambda I is exactly the derivative of the PBRF at \epsilon = 0. Bae et al. (2022) confirmed empirically that such influence estimates align well with the actual PBRF.

11.2.3 Addressing the Computational and Memory Bottlenecks of GGN#

The size of the GGN matrix G \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times p} (where p is the number of model parameters) can be extremely large for modern language models, which makes the calculation of (G + \lambda I)^{-1} computationally expensive. As a result, follow-up methods have avoided explicitly calculating this inverse matrix by borrowing techniques from the optimisation literature.

Kronecker-Factored Approximate Curvature (K-FAC).

Martens & Grosse (2015) proposed Kronecker-Factored Approximate Curvature (K-FAC) to approximate a large Fisher information matrix (FIM) using a set of smaller matrices. For a trained neural network parameterised by \theta^s, the FIM is defined as \mathbf{F} \coloneqq \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathbb{E}_{\hat{y} \sim p(\cdot \mid x_i, \theta^s)} \left[ \nabla_\theta \log p(\hat{y} \mid x_i, \theta^s) \nabla_\theta \log p(\hat{y} \mid x_i, \theta^s)^\top \right], where \hat{y} is a sample from the model’s prediction distribution on x_i. For example, suppose the task is three-class classification (c_1, c_2, c_3) and the model’s prediction distribution is \pi_i=\mathrm{softmax}(u_i^s) = (0.5, 0.3, 0.2). If a sampled label is \hat{y}=c_2, then \log p(\hat{y} \mid x_i, \theta^s) = \log 0.3.

Although K-FAC approximates the FIM rather than the GGN, these matrices are equivalent for common loss functions under suitable probabilistic assumptions. For example, with softmax cross-entropy, the expected outer product of the output log-likelihood gradients can be shown to equal the loss–output Hessian: \mathbb{E}_{\hat{y} \sim p(\cdot \mid x_i, \theta^s)} \left[ \nabla_u \log p(\hat{y} \mid u) \nabla_u \log p(\hat{y} \mid u)^\top \right]_{u=u_i^s} = \mathrm{diag}(\pi_i)-\pi_i\pi_i^\top = H_i. Applying the chain rule then gives \mathbf{F} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}J_i^\top H_i J_i = G. This shows that we can use K-FAC to approximate the GGN used in the PBRF influence score.

Now we return to K-FAC, which efficiently approximates the FIM. First, it ignores curvature between layers and approximates \mathbf{F} as a block-diagonal matrix with one block per layer. Consider a neural network in which each layer \ell has a weight matrix W_\ell \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}, input activations h_{\ell-1}, and pre-activations s_\ell=W_\ell h_{\ell-1} (irrelevant to subscript s in \theta^s). Following Grosse et al. (2023), the pseudo-gradient is defined as \mathcal{D}_{s_\ell} \coloneqq \nabla_{s_\ell} \log p(\hat{y}\mid x,\theta^s) \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}}}—the gradient flowing back to {s_\ell}. The pseudo-gradient of the layer weights is \mathcal{D}_{s_\ell} h_{\ell-1}^\top. Its vectorisation gives h_{\ell-1}\otimes\mathcal{D}_{s_\ell}, where \otimes denotes the Kronecker product. Leveraging \otimes’s properties that (A\otimes B)^\top = A^\top \otimes B^\top and (A\otimes B)(C\otimes D) = (AC)\otimes (BD), the block for this layer in the FIM, denoted as \mathbf{F}_\ell, can be represented as \mathbf{F}_\ell = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[ (h_{\ell-1}h_{\ell-1}^\top) \otimes (\mathcal{D}_{s_\ell}\mathcal{D}_{s_\ell}^\top) \right]}_{\in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{in}} d_{\text{out}}\times d_{\text{in}} d_{\text{out}}}} \approx \underbrace{ \mathbb{E}[h_{\ell-1}h_{\ell-1}^\top] }_{\eqqcolon\,\textbf{A}_{\ell-1} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{in}}\times d_{\text{in}}}} \otimes \underbrace{ \mathbb{E}[\mathcal{D}_{s_\ell}\mathcal{D}_{s_\ell}^\top] }_{\eqqcolon\,\textbf{S}_\ell \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}}\times d_{\text{out}}}}. The approximation separates the expectation into two independent factors: activations and pseudo-gradients. K-FAC thus stores only d_{\text{in}}^2+d_{\text{out}}^2 entries instead of (d_{\text{in}}d_{\text{out}})^2 entries for each block. When the two factors are invertible, their Kronecker product can be inverted separately: (\textbf{A}_{\ell-1}\otimes \textbf{S}_\ell)^{-1} = \textbf{A}_{\ell-1}^{-1}\otimes \textbf{S}_\ell^{-1}. This substantially reduces the memory required to store G and the computational cost of computing the inverse of G+\lambda I.

Eigenvalue-Corrected K-FAC (EK-FAC) and ASTRA.

EK-FAC (George et al., 2018) improved K-FAC by correcting its eigenvalues. Recent work has adopted this technique to scale the influence function method to modern LLMs (Grosse et al., 2023). ASTRA (Wang et al., 2025a) further used EK-FAC as a preconditioner for Neumann series iterations, achieving higher-quality GGN-vector products with less hyperparameter tuning. These efforts make influence functions increasingly practical for modern language models with billions of parameters.

11.2.4 Retraining-Based Influence Estimation: datamodels and TRAK#

Some methods have explored retraining the model to estimate the attribution. The most naive method is to upweight or remove each training sample, retrain the model on the modified training set, and observe changes in the model’s output on the testing sample. Ilyas et al. (2022) proposed datamodels to more efficiently estimate each training sample’s attribution on a testing sample by retraining many models on random subsets of the training data and fitting a linear regression that predicts the model output on the testing sample from binary indicators of whether each training sample was included. Each fitted coefficient then represents the training sample’s influence on the testing sample.

However, datamodels remains computationally expensive because it requires training many models. Tracing with the Randomly-projected After Kernel (TRAK; Park et al., 2023) provides a more efficient alternative. TRAK first uses a first-order Taylor expansion around the model parameters to approximate a trained model as a linear model. It then applies random projections to reduce gradient dimensionality and estimate training-sample influence in this lower-dimensional space. Finally, it averages the estimates across a small number of trained models to reduce variability due to training randomness. TRAK achieves attribution quality comparable to datamodels at substantially lower computational cost (Park et al., 2023).

11.2.5 Efficient Influence Estimation Using Training Trajectories: TracIn#

TracIn (Garima et al., 2020) estimates training-data influence using gradients evaluated along the training trajectory, without computing the inverse Hessian. Its core idea follows from the effect of a single Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD; Robbins & Monro, 1951) update on the loss of a testing sample.

Suppose the model parameters at training step t are \theta^t. An SGD update using training sample z_m with learning rate \eta_t is: \theta^{t+1} = \theta^t-\eta_t\nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}(z_m,\theta^t). By simple rearrangement, we have \theta^{t+1} - \theta^t =-\eta_t\nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}(z_m,\theta^t). Then, by first-order Taylor expansion, the change in the loss of a testing sample z_{\text{test}} can be approximated as \begin{aligned} &\mathcal{L}(z_{\text{test}},\theta^{t+1}) -\mathcal{L}(z_{\text{test}},\theta^t)\\ &\quad\approx \nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}(z_{\text{test}},\theta^t)^\top (\theta^{t+1}-\theta^t)\\ &\quad= -\eta_t \nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}(z_{\text{test}},\theta^t)^\top \nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}(z_m,\theta^t). \end{aligned} The above expression relates the gradient of the loss on a training sample z_m to the gradient on a test sample z_{\text{test}} and the change in test loss on z_{\text{test}}. A positive dot product indicates that the training update will reduce the test loss, while a negative value indicates the opposite.

TracIn extends this idea across training. Its practical checkpoint-based variant, TracInCP, estimates influence by summing gradient dot products over model checkpoints: \begin{aligned} &\operatorname{TracInCP}(z_m,z_{\text{test}})\\ &\quad= \sum_{t\in\mathcal{C}}\eta_t \nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}(z_{\text{test}},\theta^t)^\top \nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}(z_m,\theta^t), \end{aligned} where \mathcal{C} denotes the selected training checkpoints. A larger positive score indicates that z_m has contributed more towards reducing the test loss.

Unlike Hessian-based influence functions, TracIn does not require matrix inversion. However, TracInCP approximates the influence of training samples using gradients only at selected checkpoints, which may undermine the estimation accuracy.

Observation.

Influence functions identify training documents that impacted the translation, even without exact verbatim matches. This demonstrates that gradient-based methods can capture influences beyond what verbatim methods can reveal.

11.2.7 Recent Extensions and Limitations#

Recent work extends training data attribution to handle training stochasticity. Distributional TDA (d-TDA) estimates how output distributions depend on the dataset (Mlodozeniec et al., 2025). Georgiev et al. (2023) proposed TRAK variants to address diffusion models. More recent work connects TDA directly to mechanistic interpretability (Kowal et al., 2026; Habibi et al., 2026); for example, Kowal et al. (2026) introduced Concept Influence to enable attribution at the level of semantic concepts represented by model internals such as sparse autoencoder features. This brings TDA closer to the mechanistic goal of understanding how training data shapes specific internal computations. However, fundamental technical challenges persist, including approximation errors that compound over the training trajectory, variability across training runs caused by randomness like data ordering, and memory and computational bottlenecks of scaling to frontier models. These limitations motivate further research in this direction.

Summary.

Training data attribution traces model behaviour back to the training data. Verbatim methods such as OLMoTrace identify memorised spans, while gradient-based methods such as influence functions estimate the contribution of individual training samples to downstream model predictions. These approaches remain limited at scale. Meanwhile, recent work has begun linking data attribution to internal features and mechanisms. Having surveyed the main methodological families of AI interpretability in the preceding sections, Section 12 organises them into six research orientations that differ in their goals, assumptions, and paths forward.
