9 Training Data Attribution and Influence Functions#

Training data attribution (TDA) answers a fundamental interpretability question: which training examples shaped specific model behaviors? This enables identifying influential data, detecting train-test leakage, and understanding failure modes without retraining (Liu et al., 2025a; Park et al., 2023; Grosse et al., 2023). We focus on post-hoc attribution methods that analyze trained model parameters to trace behavior back to training data; data pipeline interventions such as filtering pretraining corpora, balancing demographic representation, or memorization scheduling operate prior to training and fall outside this scope.

Two complementary approaches address this question. Verbatim data attribution targets explicit memorization by identifying exact or near-exact matches between model outputs and training data, scaling this search across trillions of tokens via suffix arrays and infinite n-gram models. Gradient-based data attribution estimates subtle training influence by measuring how training points affect model parameters and predictions, capturing indirect effects that do not manifest as verbatim reproduction. Together, these methods provide orthogonal views: one answers “did the model copy this exactly?”; the other answers “how much did this training example influence the model’s behavior?”

9.1 Verbatim Data Attribution: OLMoTrace and Maximal Matching Spans#

While gradient-based methods estimate subtle training influence, verbatim data attribution focuses on explicit memorization: exact or near-exact reproductions of training data. Earlier corpus search tools such as the ROOTS Search Tool (Piktus et al., 2023a) and GAIA Search (Piktus et al., 2023b) established the practice of querying pretraining corpora for transparency and data exploration. OLMoTrace scales this to trillions of tokens using a combination of FM-index, infini-gram models, and maximal matching span extraction (Liu et al., 2025a; 2024; Xu et al., 2025), making it less a data attribution method in the influence-function sense and more a retrieval tool for identifying memorized content.

The suffix array of a string T of length N is an array of integers representing the starting positions of all suffixes of T in lexicographic order. It enables efficient substring searches in \mathcal{O}(L \cdot \log N) time for a query of length L, compared to \mathcal{O}(L \cdot N) naive search.

Example: T = \texttt{banana\$} \begin{align*} \text{Suffixes: } & 0:\texttt{banana\$}, 1:\texttt{anana\$}, 2:\texttt{nana\$}, 3:\texttt{ana\$}, 4:\texttt{na\$}, 5:\texttt{a\$}, 6:\texttt{\$} \\ \text{SA(T)} &= [6, 5, 3, 1, 0, 4, 2] \end{align*}

An infini-gram extends the n-gram model to arbitrary-length prefixes, using backoff to the longest matching sequence in the training corpus. Formally, for a token w_i: P_{\infty}(w_i \mid w_{1:i-1}) = \frac{\text{cnt}(w_{i-n+1:i} \mid \mathcal{D})}{\text{cnt}(w_{i-n+1:i-1} \mid \mathcal{D})}, \quad n = \max \{ n' : \text{cnt}(w_{i-n'+1:i-1} \mid \mathcal{D})>0\}, where w_{1:i-1} are preceding tokens and \mathcal{D} is the training corpus.

Infini-grams are implemented on top of the FM-index, which generalizes the suffix array to allow fast, memory-efficient substring queries across massive corpora.

Given a model output and a training corpus, a maximal matching span is a contiguous sequence of tokens that:

  1. Appears verbatim at least once in the training data,

  2. Does not break words or include spurious delimiters (e.g., periods or newlines) except at span boundaries, and

  3. Is not contained within a longer span that satisfies (1) and (2).

OLMoTrace identifies maximal matching spans by combining FM-index search with infini-gram queries, adjusting boundaries for tokenization, and retaining only the longest non-overlapping spans.

Prompt: “Celine Dion is a Canadian singer known for her powerful voice and wide [...]”

Pipeline Steps:

  1. Tokenize LM output.

  2. For each token starting a new word, query the FM-index with infini-gram to find the longest matching prefix in the training corpus.

  3. Adjust span boundaries to avoid breaking words or including delimiters.

  4. Retain only maximal spans (not contained in longer matches).

  3. Use infini-gram queries to determine the longest matching prefix starting at b, backing off to shorter n-grams if necessary.

  4. Adjust the match length to avoid breaking words or including spurious delimiters.

  5. Collect all spans (b, b+\text{length}) that satisfy the above criteria.

  6. Filter out spans contained within longer overlapping spans to retain only maximal matches.

9.1.1 Limitations#

Verbatim attribution excels at factual memorization but struggles with compositional or synthesized outputs. For tasks like translation, poetry, or reasoning, OLMoTrace can identify spans of text that appear in the training data, but these matches do not explain how the model combined information from multiple sources to produce the output (Park et al., 2023).

9.2 Gradient-Based Attribution: Understanding Influence Through Data Perturbations#

9.2.1 Core Idea: Parameter Response to Training Data#

Influence functions ask a counterfactual question: how would a model’s learned parameters change if we upweighted a training example (Koh & Liang, 2017)? Instead of expensive retraining, we estimate this via implicit differentiation (Grosse et al., 2023).

Consider upweighting example z by amount \epsilon in the loss: \mathcal{L}_\epsilon(z) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \mathcal{L}(z_i, \theta) + \epsilon \mathcal{L}(z, \theta).

By the implicit function theorem, if the Hessian H_{\theta^*} = \nabla^2_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{avg}}(\theta^*) is invertible, the optimal parameters \theta^*_\epsilon respond: \frac{d\theta^*_\epsilon}{d\epsilon}\Big|_{\epsilon=0} = -H_{\theta^*}^{-1} \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(z, \theta^*), where H_{\theta^*} is the Hessian of the average training loss. Applying the chain rule, the influence on a test output is: \mathcal{I}_{f}(z_m) = -\nabla_\theta f(\theta^*)^\top H_{\theta^*}^{-1} \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(z_m, \theta^*). This extends to the change in test predictions: f(\theta^*(\epsilon)) - f(\theta^*) \approx \mathcal{I}_{\text{f}}(z_m) \epsilon = -\nabla_\theta f(\theta^*)^\top H_{\theta^*}^{-1} \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(z_m, \theta^*) \epsilon. This scalar measures how upweighting z_m changes the model output on z_{\text{test}} through parameter changes.

9.2.2 Proximal Bregman Response and the Gauss-Newton Hessian#

The implicit function theorem assumes exact convergence and infinite perturbations. Real neural networks violate both: training reaches saddle points, not true minima, and we retrain for only a few steps. Rather than asking what parameters would result from full retraining, the proximal Bregman response function (PBRF) framework (Bae et al., 2022) asks: what parameters minimize the loss while staying close to the current parameters? \theta_{\text{PBRF}}(\epsilon) = \arg\min_\theta \mathcal{L}_\epsilon(z) + \frac{\lambda}{2}\|\theta - \theta_s\|^2, where \lambda acts as implicit regularization from partial retraining.

Taking the implicit function theorem through the linearization and solving this optimization problem (Bae et al., 2022) yields a remarkable result: the linearized PBRF solution is exactly the influence function estimate using the Gauss-Newton Hessian (GGN) as the curvature: G = \mathbb{E}[J_y^\top H_y J_y], where J_y is the Jacobian of the network’s outputs with respect to parameters and H_y is the Hessian of the loss with respect to the outputs. The GGN is positive semidefinite (assuming that the loss function is convex as a function of network outputs) and is the natural curvature for the PBRF problem. The influence formula becomes: \mathcal{I}_{f}(z, z_{\text{test}}) = -\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(z_{\text{test}}, \theta^*)^\top (G + \lambda I)^{-1} \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(z, \theta^*), where the \lambda I term is the implicit regularization from the PBRF.

For standard loss functions (cross-entropy, squared loss), the GGN is equivalent to the Fisher Information Matrix F = \mathbb{E}[\nabla \mathcal{L} \nabla \mathcal{L}^\top]. This equivalence allows practitioners to use Fisher information as a practical substitute: it’s more stable to compute and estimate from data than the true GGN.

9.2.3 Computational Bottleneck: Approximating GGN-Vector Products#

The main practical challenge is computing influence for each training example without explicitly forming the GGN inverse: \mathbf{v} = (G + \lambda I)^{-1} \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(z, \theta^*), which naively costs \mathcal{O}(p^2) memory and \mathcal{O}(p^3) time for p parameters. Practical methods avoid explicit inversion by using iterative solvers and structured approximations of the GGN.

Kronecker-Factored Approximate Curvature (KFAC).

KFAC is a parametric approximation to the Fisher Information Matrix (FIM) of a neural network which supports efficient inversion. The FIM is defined as: F = \mathbb{E}_{\hat{y} \sim P_{\hat{y}|x}(\theta)} \left[ \nabla_\theta \log p(\hat{y}|\theta, x) \nabla_\theta \log p(\hat{y}|\theta, x)^\top \right], where \hat{y} is sampled from the model’s output distribution (not the training labels, which would yield the empirical Fisher with less favorable properties). For neural networks, KFAC approximates this by exploiting a layer-wise Kronecker-factored structure Ḟor layer \ell with input activations a_{\ell-1} and output gradient components (Grosse et al., 2023), KFAC approximates: F_\ell \approx (a_{\ell-1} a_{\ell-1}^\top) \otimes (D_s^\top D_s), where \otimes is the Kronecker product and D_s represents the output gradient structure. This factorization reduces memory from \mathcal{O}(p^2) to \mathcal{O}(p) and enables efficient layer-wise solves. KFAC works well but can underestimate curvature in low-variance directions due to finite sampling.

Eigenvalue-Corrected KFAC (EK-FAC) and ASTRA.

EK-FAC improves KFAC by correcting eigenvalues and is the method used by recent work scaling influence to LLMs (Grosse et al., 2023). An even more recent refinement, ASTRA (Wang et al., 2025a), uses EK-FAC as a preconditioner for Neumann series iterations, yielding higher-quality GGN-vector products with less hyperparameter tuning. These structured approaches make influence functions practical for models with billions of parameters.

9.2.4 Unrolled Differentiation: Gradient-Based Attribution#

A complementary approach bypasses the Hessian entirely. Approximate unrolled differentiation (Bae et al., 2024) unfolds the optimization trajectory and backpropagates through training steps. This captures implicit bias of the optimizer and handles non-converged models, where classical influence functions struggle. The tradeoff: higher computational cost than influence functions but better accuracy on modern, non-convex objectives.

9.2.5 Practical Attribution at Scale: TRAK#

For large-scale attribution without retraining thousands of models, TRAK (Tracing with Randomly-projected After Kernel) provides a scalable alternative (Park et al., 2023). TRAK combines three key ideas: a first-order Taylor expansion approximating how model behavior changes under data perturbations, random projections to reduce gradient dimensionality from p parameters to k \ll p, and training only a handful of models on different data subsets rather than retraining exhaustively. By avoiding prohibitive retraining costs, TRAK achieves attribution quality comparable to expensive retraining-based methods like datamodels (Ilyas et al., 2022) while reducing computational cost by orders of magnitude. This enables application to large-scale models including vision transformers and billion-parameter language models, making fine-grained training data attribution tractable for modern deep learning (Park et al., 2023).

9.2.6 Efficient Influence Estimation: TracIn#

TracIn provides an efficient alternative to computing the full Hessian inverse by leveraging the training trajectory (Pruthi et al., 2020). Rather than computing influence at convergence via H_{\theta^*}^{-1} \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(z, \theta^*), TracIn approximates influence by integrating the gradient of a test example’s loss with respect to gradients along the training path: \text{influence}(z_{\text{train}}, z_{\text{test}}) \approx \sum_{t=1}^T \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(z_{\text{test}}, \theta_t) \cdot \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(z_{\text{train}}, \theta_t) where \theta_t denotes parameters at training step t. This approach avoids Hessian computation entirely, instead accumulating dot products of gradients throughout training. TracIn is considerably cheaper than influence functions based on Hessian approximations, making it practical for large models. However, the approximation assumes that gradient trajectories during training encode sufficient information about final influence, an assumption that may break down in highly nonlinear settings or when training dynamics involve significant noise (Pruthi et al., 2020). Despite limitations, TracIn has enabled attribution studies on modern language models and provides a useful baseline for scalable influence estimation.

9.2.8 Recent Extensions and Limitations#

Recent work extends training data attribution to handle training stochasticity. Distributional TDA (d-TDA) predicts how output distributions depend on the dataset (Mlodozeniec et al., 2025), while TRAK variants address diffusion models (Georgiev et al., 2023). More recent work connects TDA directly to mechanistic interpretability: (Kowal et al., 2026) introduce Concept Influence, which enables attribution at the level of individual model components rather than raw parameters, bringing TDA closer to the mechanistic goal of understanding how training data shapes specific internal computations. However, fundamental challenges persist: approximation errors compound over optimization trajectories, random hyperparameters introduce variance across runs, and scaling to larger models remains memory-intensive. These obstacles suggest that fully resolving attribution at scale may require fundamentally new approaches.
